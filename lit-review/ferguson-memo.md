## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo analyzes Ferguson's article, as it relates to the broader framing issue that has guided this part of the literature review. In what ways are trade secrets protected as "investments" (under international investment law and investor-State arbitration), and what specific legal protections do investors have against a host state that threatens to disclose, regulate, or use their trade secret? The question was written with the intention of being split into two. The first part of the question is essentially a definitional and jurisdictional question about whether a trade secret would meet the definition of an "investment" and therefore qualify for ISDS protection at all. The second portion of the question is much more substantive and focuses on which doctrines exist to protect an investor after they have qualified for jurisdictional protection from the host state, but the host state has already disclosed, used, or regulated the secret. There are very few pieces in the current body of literature that consider both parts of the question separately; thus, I felt it necessary to create a dedicated memo for Ferguson's piece within a multifaceted research question forum.

## II. Source Overview
### A. Argument and Framework
The primary basis of Mr. Ferguson's position is that Trade Secrets are the most vulnerable type of Intellectual Property, and therefore require protection as "Investments" within modern Bilateral Investment Treaties (BITs) and International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes ("ISDS"), as well as the doctrine of indirect expropriation without compensation — which will serve as the best method to protect against the four key risks posed by an action of a State towards a foreign investor's Secret, which include: disclosure; use; regulation; or a combination of these. Ferguson develops this position using two analytical steps. First, he outlines a process of defining what constitutes an "investment," starting with the reference to Article 39 of the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS), continuing to define the trade secret as recognized at the local level, and finally concluding with how it defines "investment" in the context of BITs, while using Article 31(3)(c) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) to incorporate TRIPS as an additional rule of international law binding upon the Parties. Second, Mr. Ferguson outlines a four-part inquiry to determine whether an action of a State towards a foreign investor's trade secret is considered an act of indirect expropriation. The four inquiries ask: (1) Is the state protection absolute? (2) Has there been significant interference in the legal protection afforded to the trade secret? (3) Has there been significant interference in the exclusive commercial use of the trade secret? (4) Has there been a substantial reduction in the commercial value of the trade secret?

### B. Case Selection and Scope
Although the article's subject matter is very comprehensive from a legal standpoint, Ferguson has limited his focus when it comes to the use of cases in order to define this area of study. Ferguson addresses the trade secret laws of the U.S., the E.U. and the U.K. at the initial recognition phase; however, when he applies international arbitration standards to those same laws, he continues to rely heavily upon just a handful of tribunals, including Tethyan Copper Co. v. Pakistan, Saluka v. Czech Republic, Bayview Irrigation v. Mexico and the historic USA v. Peru (Landreau) award. Ferguson also includes supplemental information provided by several World Trade Organization Panel decisions made in Thailand-Cigarettes and Argentina-Bovine Hides. In addition to limiting himself to only expropriation-type claims regarding trade secret disputes, Ferguson excludes all other types of claims such as Fair and Equitable Treatment and Umbrella Clause Claims as being "an issue for another time." This approach has clearly sharpened Ferguson's expropriation analysis; however, it limits the broader potential of this study. Unlike Kim's piece, which defines trade secrets first and foremost as a type of Intellectual Property Law, and then considers how those laws can be used as an Asset Class under International Arbitration Standards, Ferguson views trade secrets first and foremost as an Asset Class under International Arbitration Standards and uses Intellectual Property Laws to support those views.

## IV. Substantive Analysis
### A. Risk Architecture
The threat landscape facing a foreign investor's trade secret can be organized by Ferguson into four different forms of State conduct: disclosure, use, regulation, and combinations of these three. The classification method used by Ferguson is atypical because it is based on function (what the State actually did) rather than on procedure or evidence (both of which would arise later in the analysis), and it is ordered according to how the State engages with the secret. Disclosure captures how the State exposes the secret to the public through its apparatus for transparency. Use becomes relevant when the State, or a State-owned entity, uses a trade secret for commercial gain against a foreign investor, as Ferguson identifies in Saluka and the Dutch glass factory archetype. Regulation becomes relevant when the State requires the owner of the secret to provide information about the secret so that they can have access to a regulated market, and this is where Ferguson identifies the pharmaceuticals and tobacco ingredient archetypes. What makes Ferguson's classification method useful for this research project is his recognition that trade secrets are particularly vulnerable to normal State activity compared to all other intellectual property rights. As Ferguson explains, a State is much more likely to expose a trade secret during its normal activities than it is to exploit a trademark; thus, threats to trade secrets are both more numerous and more likely to occur than threats to all other categories of intellectual property.

### B. Recognition / Enforceability Gap
There is another structural risk in addition to the four risk categories, and that is the difference in how the host state recognizes the secrecy of the investment and how it can be enforced via international law. Ferguson's argument is that international law doesn't generate trade secret protections for investments; instead, international law protects those areas in an investment that are already recognized by the host state's municipal laws. Thus, when a trade secret investor does not have legal protection of their trade secret in the host state, they do not have an "investment" to protect, even though the exact same trade secret could have been protected and valued in the investor's own country. Therefore, from a double-contingency perspective, the investor's secret must be recognized and validated by the municipal laws of the host state, and then it must meet the definition of "investment" prior to the substance of the expropriation doctrine ever being engaged with. Ferguson views this aspect of the system as a benefit because it preserves State sovereignty over the definitional question.

### C. Evidentiary Standards
Ferguson has a very limited discussion of the evidentiary structure; while I believe this is an omission as opposed to a deliberate decision by Ferguson, he identifies the key issue (i.e., that proving the existence of a trade secret destroys its value) and mentions that both WIPO and UNCITRAL have had a hard time with this issue for years. However, he does not follow this up with a detailed analysis of the in-camera review, protective orders, or redacted publication of awards. His apparent unstated position is that the evidentiary machinery of current arbitral practice suffices for the proof-of-recognition phase, and that the harder issues reside within the substantive law rather than the procedural law. This is where Ferguson is weakest, and where the research project will need to seek additional support from outside of the substantive-doctrine literature, most probably in the form of the procedural-rules literature, including Aceris Law's commentary on confidentiality orders (practitioner commentary) and the IBA and ICC guidelines on protecting trade secrets in arbitration.

### D. Pharmaceutical Data and Regulatory Disclosure
Ferguson does engage pharmaceutical test data, but obliquely, and primarily through the tobacco ingredient fact-pattern and through the lens of regulation aimed at achieving a "public welfare" (health) goal. He also doesn't mention Vanda Pharmaceuticals or the FDA Disclosure cases by name — an omission that was unfortunate, given that this piece was published before the case began, and since the Vanda case clearly illustrates each of his four questions. However, the article does a good job of framing the inherent tension of the issue. In order to achieve its public welfare objectives (such as health), the state may need to force the owner of a trade secret to reveal the secret, and after the information has been revealed, the government agency can then act on that information to enforce regulations without having to compensate the original holder. This process devalues the trade secret, but Ferguson frames it as a legitimate application of police power. Ferguson's implied response to the question of whether ISDS will provide protections to assets whose value is based upon their secrecy is a qualified "yes", i.e., ISDS will protect those assets through the expropriation provisions if Ferguson's four question framework determines that the loss of commercial exclusivity represents a material impact (i.e. a substantial interference); and, if the actions taken by the state cannot be reframed as a genuine attempt to regulate. The qualifier matters because many regulatory schemes (including those concerning disclosure requirements) are specifically intended to appear as though they represent a genuine attempt to regulate; and thus, the amount of actual protection that Ferguson can realistically provide to pharmaceutical investors is narrower than what could potentially be provided under the theory.

### E. Reforms / Position Within the Benavides Project
Ferguson has no plans to revise treaties, amend institutions, nor create additional procedural mechanisms; therefore, his is an interpretative, rather than legislative, project. Furthermore, Ferguson believes the current investment treaty regime, interpreted via the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) and the TRIPS Agreement, contains all the needed doctrinal support to protect trade secrets. In addition, he believes that the four questions he has proposed are merely a restatement of what courts have always done when assessing whether there was expropriatory action, and thus they do not represent calls for new law. If I can identify anything approaching a reform proposal from the article, it is his idea that the analysis within the substantial interference prong of the expropriation test should center around the trade secret itself and not upon the entire scope of the investor's investment. This represents a significant expansion in protections available to investors who possess specialized trade-secret holdings as opposed to those with diversified investments. Another important aspect of this study relates to the fact that Ferguson had identified areas where there were clear gaps in the system, however, he located all solutions solely in the realm of doctrine. Thus, this is ultimately a wager on the ability of arbitrators to be persuaded as much as the research project wishes.

## V. Relationship to Kim
### A. Points of Disagreement with Kim
Ferguson adds to Daria Kim's approach, with respect to the area of operation where Kim is most weak, namely the operationalization of trade secrets within ISDS. He also contests Kim with respect to the area of operation where both scholars have differing prior beliefs concerning the extent to which treaty language may be used to complete the requisite amount of doctrinal development. For example, whereas Kim views the failure of current treaties to provide adequate trade secret protection in ISDS as a structural issue requiring treaty reform, Ferguson views this deficiency as an interpretative problem that is resolvable by tribunals using superior decision-making. This disagreement is genuine and therefore the research project will either need to side with Ferguson or reject his conclusions without being able to "split the baby." I believe at present that Ferguson has the best legal argument regarding what doctrine will permit trade secret protection to be provided via tribunals (i.e., through VCLT Article 31(3)(c), supplemented by TRIPS). However, I also think that he overstates the political aspect of his claim since there is little evidence to suggest that tribunals are willing to engage in such broad-scope systematising; nor does he adequately show that the case law cited in support of his contention supports those claims based on holdings rather than mere dicta.

### B. Points of Development Beyond Kim
Where Ferguson most clearly develops upon Kim is through the four-risk taxonomy and the four-question expropriation test. Neither of these appears in Kim's monograph in this form. Both of these are directly responsive to the questions Dr. Benavides has asked me since our first meeting (i.e., what "use" looks like in practice, what "disclosure" looks like in practice, and what tribunals should look for to tell whether an asset is being used as a regulatory tool or a confiscatory one if the asset is purely informational). The second major development of Ferguson's work toward the project is his identification of the recognition/enforceability gap in Part II.B. This creates a solid analytical construct that explains why trade secrets are uniquely vulnerable to theoretical erosion relative to other forms of intellectual property rights. The development that Ferguson does not contribute to, and which the project will need to develop itself, is a detailed examination of the procedural and evidence-based mechanisms of establishing that there exists a trade secret without causing its destruction.

## VI. Strengths and Weaknesses
### A. Strengths
In terms of contribution, Ferguson makes the greatest contribution with his four-question test for indirect expropriation. He has developed the rare combination of being both analytical (theoretically) and operational (practically), in the form that he has put together the four questions, and an arbitrator, in my opinion, could pick them up tomorrow without needing to rewrite the relevant treaty. These questions are well-ordered, moving from absoluteness, to legal protection, to exclusive use, to commercial value. Most importantly, the answers to all of these questions correspond to existing jurisprudence on substantial interference, so that when an arbitrator answers the questions using the substantial-interference jurisprudence that already exists under international law, he will not need to create any new jurisprudence.

### B. Weaknesses
Ferguson's weakest argument is his treatment of the "territorial nexus" discussed in Part II. He makes an arguably reasonable claim that capital is "relevantly allocated" to a trade secret when that capital is allocated to the disclosure or use of the trade secret in the host State. However, given how much weight this statement will be required to carry as a connection between the recognition argument and the investment argument, the amount of supporting authority provided by Ferguson is insufficient. As such, while the reference to Aplin et al. provides some support for the proposition, and the UK breach-of-confidence analogy found in footnote 114 is likely providing more rhetorical than legal force, a skeptical tribunal may find no obligation to follow Ferguson and the remainder of the analytical structure would suffer accordingly. The research project should consider this the weakest link in Ferguson's argument and seek additional evidence (possibly from the European Trade Secrets Directive; or from recent Court of Justice of the European Union decisions concerning territorial applicability of trade secret protections).

### C. Open Questions for the Project
Three open questions for the project. First: does the four-question test survive contact with a tribunal that holds an aggressive view of police powers? Ferguson concedes in Part V that A11Y and Burlington both went the State's way on indirect expropriation grounds, but does not work out what his test would have done in those cases. Second: how should the project handle Tethyan Copper, which Ferguson uses as his most recent and most useful tribunal data point, but it is partly inaccessible in its unredacted form? Third, and most consequential for Dr. Benavides specifically: is the recognition–enforceability gap a feature or a bug? Ferguson treats it as a feature (it preserves State sovereignty over the definitional question); a more critical reading would treat it as the structural reason ISDS protection of trade secrets is illusory in the very States most likely to misuse them. In my opinion, this research project should look into this and take a position.
