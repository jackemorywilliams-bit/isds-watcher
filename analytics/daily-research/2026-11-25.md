### 6.8 Where this leaves the research question, in plain language

The project is looking for one particular kind of lawsuit: a company sues a country before an international
tribunal; it says that something it owns in the realm of ideas — a patent, a trademark, and above all a
trade secret or a package of clinical-trial data — counts as an "investment" the country promised to
protect; it says a government decision or a court ruling destroyed that thing; and then it argues with the
country about whether the tribunal may hear the case at all. **That lawsuit has still not been found, and
nothing today brought it closer.**

The council re-examined the same eighteen items the instrument collected on 24 November, and the answer is
unchanged: **none of them is that lawsuit and none is halfway there.** Eleven were specialist arbitration
headlines about awards being enforced, annulled or set aside and about disputes over gold mines, real
estate, water concessions, a drone programme and poultry tariffs — real investor-State litigation with no
intellectual property in it. Three were notices about disputes between countries, with no company involved.
Two were academic papers on trade secrets, neither involving a country, a treaty or a tribunal. One was a
practitioner's guide to how Belgium protects pharmaceutical regulatory data: **the project's own software
read it, found exactly the vocabulary the project was built to find, and recorded that there was no dispute
of any kind in it.** The last was a stock-market press release.

**The one genuinely new thing learned today is about the project's own eyesight, and the council got its
direction wrong before it got it right — so this paragraph, and not the analyst's memo, is what the council
holds.** The instrument records separately, for each item, whether a real arbitration was identified and
whether the asset vocabulary scored. The analyst found those two judgements landing on completely different
items and suggested the gap might be a shadow cast by which sources the project can read. **Tested against
every run the project has ever recorded, that suggestion is wrong, and the truth points the other way.**

vocabulary scored, and not one item is in both groups.** One source — the IISD investment-treaty news
service — produces **both** kinds of item, so this is not a matter of which doors happen to be open. **The
project's own records say the two worlds are further apart than this council had measured, not closer.**
The caution that survives is narrower and worth stating plainly: most of those reads are the first few
thousand characters of a case or docket page rather than of a pleading, and **a case page that does not
mention a trade secret is not evidence that no trade secret was pleaded.**
