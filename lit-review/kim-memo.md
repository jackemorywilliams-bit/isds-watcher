## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo will analyze Kim's article based on the original framing question that you handed me at the beginning of this review, namely: do the prospects for investor-state arbitration (ISDS) for a pharmaceutical company's clinical trial test data truly reflect the negative prospects for the investor that Kim indicates they have, and if so why? Kim is the sole source referenced in the reading materials where she has addressed the pharmaceutical disclosure fact pattern on its own terms versus using the pharmaceutical disclosure as just another example in a broad trade secret legal framework. Additionally, while Ferguson is positive that the indirect expropriation doctrine, as interpreted through TRIPS and VCLT, could provide sufficient protections for trade secrets under ISDS, Kim believes otherwise, and that skepticism is directly attributed to the specific regulatory environment of the pharmaceutical industry that the research project is most concerned about. Therefore, I treated Kim as the primary counterpoint to Ferguson, and reviewed her article with focus on what aspects of the clinical-trial data fact pattern she believed would make ISDS protection unlikely.

## II. Source Overview
### A. Argument and Framework
Kim argues that even though pharmaceutical companies could (in theory) make an investment dispute out of their complaints about domestic government regulatory requirements that demand disclosure of clinical trial data, there is little real prospect of success with investor-state arbitration regarding such demands. This is due to the inherent characteristics of clinical trial data as a product of research, and the public welfare nature of the regulatory frameworks which provide for disclosure. In two steps, similar to those taken by Ferguson, Kim arrives at the contrary position. In the first step, Kim addresses the threshold issue of whether clinical trial data constitutes an "investment" within the terms of the various investment treaties and the ICSID Convention, using the Apotex v. United States award and the Philip Morris v. Uruguay case as reference points while noting that neither provides clear-cut conclusions. In the second step, Kim identifies the two most probable substantive standards (indirect expropriation and fair and equitable treatment) and concludes that both are unlikely to support investor claims against regulatory disclosure of clinical trial data for public health or policy innovation reasons.

### B. Case Selection and Scope
Kim's case-base is much smaller than Ferguson's and deals with only a few pharmaceutical-related and pharmaceutical adjacent issues. There are two awards (Apotex v. United States & Philip Morris v. Uruguay) which form Kim's load-bearing ISDS cases; the use of Apotex v. United States is intended by Kim to identify the "outer limits" of the term "investment" within a pharmaceutical context. The second award (Philip Morris v. Uruguay) demonstrates how tribunals will interpret the contribution-to-development criterion where an investor's activity in a host country results in a net-negative effect upon the host economy and where the host State resists jurisdiction. Additionally, Kim refers to Eli Lilly v. Canada as providing additional insight into investment claims based on patentability criteria rather than confidentiality or disclosure criteria. Kim also draws on the European Medicines Agency clinical data disclosure policy, and a series of cases decided by the European General Court and Court of Justice as to whether pharmaceutical companies could prevent the EMA from releasing clinical dossiers to the public under EU transparency regulations, namely the InterMune, AbbVie, and PTC Therapeutics litigation. Because Kim wrote in 2016, her work did not cover Vanda Pharmaceuticals v. FDA, which has evolved into the most doctrinally interesting U.S. test case dealing with similar factual circumstances; nor did she take account of the CJEU's final pronouncement on the EMA transparency cases, meaning that some of the open questions she identifies have been addressed since her writing.

## III. Sources Surveyed / Research Methods
### A. Sources Surveyed
My focus was on Part II, which analyzes how clinical trial data is treated as subject matter for the purpose of protection through the Apotex and Philip Morris case studies; and Part III, which applies the expropriation and fair and equitable treatment (FET) standards to a regulatory-disclosure factual scenario and contains Kim's substantive claim that the chances of an investor successfully bringing an investor-state arbitration over such disclosure appear to be quite low. After completing my readings of Parts II and III, I verified that Kim's overall thesis from her introduction and conclusion was reflected in what she did in Parts II and III. I focused heavily on Part III.B, particularly Kim's analysis of the EMA disclosure policy. Additionally, when I reviewed the PDF version of the article using the sticky notes I left during the first reading, there were several instances in Part III.B where Kim appeared to omit the actual decision made in the litigation she referenced in developing her argument, and since this was a pattern of omission I had identified during my first reading, I wished to verify it through my backsourcing process prior to accepting it.

### B. Research Methods
I followed the same method of using back-sourcing for further insight as we discussed at our first meeting and was able to use a digital auditing process through Claude Opus 4.6 to allow me to focus my manual verification time on the footnotes that made specific interpretative claims within the article rather than verifying each citation in the article. I also used artificial intelligence (AI) to compare Kim's citations against the official repository websites for all of the referenced documents instead of using only the abstracted information provided in the article. Using my manual annotations as the source of truth, I found three types of reference that would justify doing the backsourcing: references linked to the definitional debate regarding whether clinical trial data constitutes an "investment" under Article 25(1) of the ICSID Convention and under BIT investment definitions; references linking to the arbitral and judicial decisions cited by Kim for authority on her expropriation and FET analyses (the Apotex award and the EMA transparency decisions); and references to secondary sources on pharmaceutical data protection and exclusivity and TRIPS Article 39(3). I manually validated these references by obtaining the full text of each reference using professional databases that host the cited materials: italaw for the ISDS awards (Apotex, Philip Morris v. A number of items I initially noted with sticky notes were flagged because Kim either quoted language that does not appear to have a corresponding footnote tracing back to a decision she referenced, or cited a case for a particular proposition while failing to tell the reader what the outcome was; accordingly, I list these items in the annotated bibliography as pending backsourcing tasks prior to treating Kim's portrayal of those decisions as settled. The two items I was unable to retrieve in their entirety in this pass were the ultimate disposition of PTC Therapeutics' appeal in its transparency action and the specific section of Philip Morris v. ## IV. Substantive Analysis
### A. Clinical Data as the Subject Matter of Protection
Kim begins by arguing that clinical data is structurally unique when compared to other forms of intellectual property, and she believes that this structural uniqueness matters for the very first step of the ISDS process — determining whether the clinical data constitutes an "investment" (and therefore is subject to ISDS). Unlike patents and trademarks, which are exclusively owned and therefore easy to apply to the definitions found in BITs, clinical data is only afforded protection against "unfair commercial use" pursuant to Article 39 of the TRIPS Agreement, provided the clinical data meets the standards found in Article 39. Therefore, according to Kim, a tribunal involved in an investor-state dispute regarding clinical data must expend significantly more effort than in a patent dispute to determine whether a protectable asset even exists; furthermore, Kim argues that tribunals in practice have consistently failed to make the necessary efforts. She uses the Apotex award as her clearest evidence of this failure. In Apotex, the UNCITRAL tribunal held that a Canadian generic manufacturer's activities in the U.S. market did not constitute an investment within the meaning of NAFTA Article 1139; accordingly, the tribunal did not need to address the substantive issue of expropriation in order to dispose of the case. My read of this move is that it is analytically sound, but that it depends on a narrower definition of "investment" than Ferguson assumes, and therefore the research project will be required to assess whether Apotex is indicative of tribunal practice generally or simply a result of the specific way Apotex structured its claim. Kim also examines the application of the contribution-to-development criterion from the Salini line of decisions (as a second and connected move) in order to illustrate that even where an investor has satisfied the primary threshold of establishing an investment protected by the BIT, tribunals nonetheless possess considerable discretion with respect to whether the claimed investment constitutes the type of activity the BIT was intended to cover. This move is anchored in her examination of the Philip Morris v. Uruguay decision, and she utilizes the tribunal's refusal to construe the Salini criteria as being jurisdictionally determinative as evidence to support the proposition that the development-contribution analysis serves as a de facto discretionary backstop, to be employed by tribunals who wish to deny jurisdiction in politically sensitive cases involving challenged regulatory conduct while avoiding addressing the merits of those challenges. I consider this portion of the article to be Kim's most politically pragmatic section, because it captures how tribunals will frequently seek out jurisdictional exits in highly politicized cases even though there is no strict doctrinal obligation to utilize the Salini criteria in that manner.

### B. The Assessment Under the Expropriation Standard
Kim's approach to the application of the indirect expropriation standard is both the most clear-cut departure from Ferguson and the most direct response to Dr. Benavides' framing question. Specifically, she states that when an investor challenges a regulatory disclosure measure based upon the expropriation standard, it will nearly always be difficult for them to meet their evidentiary burden — the investor must show that the regulatory body has caused actual or possible monetary harm from non-commercial utilization of the disclosed data. This can only be done via a counterfactual damages calculation that clinical data does not easily lend itself to. The investor's theory of loss therefore has to rest on speculative downstream impacts on its market position rather than on any direct commercial exploitation of the data by the recipient of the disclosure. As Kim points out, the purpose behind making clinical trial dossiers publicly available via third parties (for example the EMA disclosure policy) is to allow review of those trials for public health purposes or to enable follow-on innovation, and by definition those uses are not commercial. Therefore, according to Kim, a skeptical tribunal would likely treat the disclosure as an ordinary regulatory action falling within the police-powers carve-out, and would consequently treat the investor's loss as a non-compensable regulatory consequence rather than a substantial deprivation of the investment. In my opinion, this is the single strongest piece of analysis in her article and also the best answer to the research question about why ISDS is unfavorable for pharmaceutical investors in this particular fact pattern, in the sense that the structural obstacle is not the doctrine itself but the evidentiary architecture that the doctrine requires an investor to satisfy.

### C. The Assessment Under the FET Standard and "Legitimate Expectations"
Kim's treatment of the fair and equitable treatment (FET) standard was where I anticipated some degree of softness with regard to doctrine. Instead, her analysis provides a tighter articulation than I expected. According to Kim, the most common form of a claim based upon a breach of the FET standard in the context of pharmaceutical disclosure would be the claimant's "legitimate expectations" that its clinical data would remain confidential. However, she states that since there are no express provisions contained within treaties, nor any regulatory assurances or customary administrative practices in place that establish an obligation to maintain confidentiality, the legitimate-expectations doctrine is too vague to support a viable claim, and tribunals have generally refused to imply obligations of confidentiality solely by virtue of general regulatory norms. The TRIPS Article 39(3) regime is applicable to this issue inasmuch as it represents the closest approximation of an enforceable obligation on States at the international level to protect pharmaceutical test data. Kim argues, however, that TRIPS Article 39(3) is constructed as an obligation to prevent unfair commercial use of data rather than as a general non-disclosure obligation. Furthermore, according to Kim, the WTO panel and legislative history support the reading that TRIPS Article 39(3) does not include regulation-based disclosures for public health reasons. In my view, this is the most carefully articulated doctrine-related portion of the article; additionally, it is the area where Kim's work differs the most from Ferguson in terms of doctrine — whereas Ferguson views TRIPS Article 39(3) as part of a bridge doctrine allowing tribunals to provide protection for trade secrets through the expropriation standard, Kim views the same provision as an obstacle to a similar FET claim.

### D. Proportionality, Police Powers, and the Public-Welfare Carve-Out
In relation to Kim's article, the research project will be looking most closely at Part III.B.2 because it goes through the proportionality test and the balancing of interests (i.e., the tests used by tribunals) to separate non-compensable regulatory conduct from compensable regulatory conduct. Kim's analysis is consistent with the standard four-step proportionality framework applied in international investment and EU regulatory law (as formalized by scholars such as Bücheler and Kingsbury & Schill, both of whom Kim cites): whether a state's action pursues a legitimate public objective, whether the method of achieving that goal is suitable, whether there are less restrictive ways of achieving that goal, and whether the benefits of the public interest achieved through the state's actions outweigh the burdens imposed upon the investor. Kim does not walk through these steps as a numbered test; rather, she addresses the necessity and proportionality questions discursively, but her analysis tracks each element. As a result, Kim believes that in almost every situation a clinical data disclosure regime established by a state to protect public health, promote medical innovation, or reduce duplication of trial results would satisfy these criteria. Therefore, even if an investor can get past the jurisdictional and evidentiary issues mentioned in previous sections, the backstop of proportionality provides tribunals with a rational legal justification to support their decision to rule in favor of the state in virtually every dispute regarding pharmaceutical data disclosure. I believe that this is the clearest explanation in the literature of why the research question has the uncomfortable answer it has, and it is also likely to be the most important way in which the project will critically address Ferguson, since Ferguson's confidence in the ability of the expropriation discipline to protect pharmaceutical trade secrets assumes that tribunals will frequently decline to apply precisely the police-powers and proportionality standards that Kim expects them to apply.

### E. What Kim's Argument Leaves Out
There are two areas in the paper where I believe Kim undercuts her own position relative to how she presents it, and both warrant the attention of the research project rather than being inherited uncritically. First, there are several of the cases cited by Kim in support of her adverse-prospects position for which she does not provide a description of what ultimately happened. My independent backsourcing indicates that in at least two of those cases (and perhaps others), the final disposition on appeal or on subsequent motions is less clear-cut than Kim characterizes the case to be. I would not go so far as to characterize this as selective citation; however, it represents a point in the argument where the reader will not necessarily verify the full procedural history of the cases prior to relying upon Kim's summary, and therefore the research project should perform its own verification of the procedural histories cited by Kim prior to accepting her characterization of each case. Second, Kim's writing predates the Vanda Pharmaceuticals v. FDA litigation and the subsequent Court of Justice decisions regarding transparency at the EMA, and while Kim identifies several open doctrinal questions which she expects will be resolved through further litigation, they have in fact been resolved since, in ways she could not reasonably have anticipated. While I do not intend to criticize Kim for writing her paper in 2016, the research project will need to incorporate a procedure to confirm whether the post-2016 case law has affected any of the doctrinal pieces that Kim treats as settled.

## V. Relationship to Ferguson
### A. Points of Disagreement with Ferguson
The debate between Kim and Ferguson is real, and it is the biggest conflict the research project has surfaced so far during this phase of the literature review. At the doctrinal level, Kim and Ferguson have examined almost identical treaty architecture — BITs, the ICSID Convention, TRIPS Article 39, and the VCLT interpretive tools — and have come to opposing conclusions. Ferguson believes that with careful application, the existing architecture provides sufficient protections for pharmaceutical trade secrets under the indirect expropriation provisions, and that the failure of tribunals to provide such protections up until now is based on an interpretive inertia that may be overcome through better arbitral reasoning. Kim, on the other hand, reads the architecture as favoring the pharmaceutical investor in no way whatsoever; she argues that there is a systematic disadvantage to investors baked into the interplay between the investor-protection backstops contained within the treaties, which all tend to move in the same direction. She also argues that while tribunals have failed to provide adequate protections for pharmaceutical investors, this is not simply a case of tribunals failing to apply the existing treaty language properly, but rather that the existing treaty structure was never designed to produce those outcomes. My preliminary read is that neither Kim nor Ferguson are actually debating what the treaties state regarding pharmaceutical trade secret protections; instead, they are debating how much leeway should be attributed to tribunals when interpreting treaty language, and whether arbitrators are able or willing to perform the kind of integrative work across multiple treaties that Kim believes they have repeatedly refused to do. The research project will need to decide where it stands on this question and not leave it unresolved. However, based on a fair examination of the cases since 2016, the honest position is closer to Kim than to Ferguson on the politics, even if Ferguson is closer to the right answer on the pure doctrinal pathway. Ferguson's doctrinal advantage is that he correctly identifies the textual tools available within the VCLT and TRIPS that would, if applied as written, permit tribunals to integrate trade secret protections into the expropriation framework; the architecture does support the reading he gives it. Where Ferguson falls short is in assuming that tribunals will actually perform that integrative work when the political cost of doing so in a pharmaceutical case is high, and Kim's proportionality analysis explains why they will not.

### B. Points of Convergence and Development
Where Kim and Ferguson actually agree, and where the research project can treat the convergence as load-bearing, is on the prior point that clinical data is fundamentally distinct from other forms of intellectual property in ways that impact the jurisdictional threshold at the ISDS level. Both scholars recognize the recognition-and-enforceability issue Ferguson identifies as part of his explicit Part II.B argument and that Kim approaches indirectly through the Apotex and Philip Morris analysis, and both scholars recognize that the TRIPS Article 39(3) regime is the textual centerpiece of any legitimate investor-protection based theory of pharmaceutical data protection. The contribution Kim makes to Ferguson, which the research project should view as her most valuable addition, is her detailed discussion of the proportionality test and the public-welfare carve-out. Ferguson recognizes them as limitations to his framework, but he does not analyze the manner in which these provisions would apply in an actual pharmaceutical dispute. Kim demonstrates, in considerable detail, why she believes they are likely to be determinative rather than simply limiting. The area in which Kim has made no such contribution, and one that the research project will still have to seek outside of Kim's work, is a comparable level of detail concerning the procedural and evidentiary mechanisms involved in successfully advancing an ISDS action regarding trade secrets without exposing the secret by doing so; this is the same deficiency I previously identified in the Ferguson memo.

## VI. Strengths and Weaknesses
### A. Strengths
Kim's best contribution is the proportionality analysis in Part III.B.2. It is the part of the article that most directly answers the framing question Dr. Benavides organized my reading around, and it is articulated with technical care rather than rhetorical flair. Kim's discursive application of the proportionality framework to the disclosure requirements imposed upon pharmaceutical companies by the EMA tracks a real and established feature of international investment arbitration as well as EU regulatory law, and her methodical treatment of the necessity and balancing questions provides a sufficient basis for another researcher or arbitrator to use her analysis as a starting point for a new fact pattern without having to rebuild the analytic framework. That kind of practical applicability is rare in the academic literature, and it is the primary reason the article remains viable nearly a decade after its original publication. Additionally, Kim's argument regarding the evidentiary burden placed on the pharmaceutical claimant under the expropriation standard is tightly reasoned and is substantiated through reference to the specific structural features of clinical trial data rather than through general claims about deference to regulatory agencies; that makes it a much more analytically useful tool for the research project than the more abstract police-powers literature the research project has otherwise relied on.

### B. Weaknesses
Kim's most glaring weak point, in my view, is her selective treatment of case outcomes that I identified in Section IV.E above. In several places throughout the article Kim cites a case to establish the proposition that tribunals have been reluctant to protect clinical data; however, in those same instances, she fails to advise the reader whether the decision was based upon the rationale cited by Kim as evidence of such reluctance, or whether the court determined the issue on a narrower ground that would be inconsistent with her argument. In other words, while she is not misrepresenting the courts' decisions, she is creating rhetorical momentum that moves beyond what the underlying record supports, and a diligent reader doing backsourcing can identify areas of the article where the evidence has left some of these questions unanswered. A further weakness is one of vintage rather than of reasoning: the article is from 2016, and the specific EMA transparency line Kim relies upon has since been adjudicated at the Court of Justice level in a manner that both supports and complicates her position. The research project should therefore consider Kim's 2016 analysis as a good starting point and not a final conclusion regarding the European side of the story.

### C. Open Questions for the Project
Three open questions for the project arise from this memo. First, can Kim's proportionality-and-police-powers answer be maintained within the post-2016 judicial landscape governing the EMA transparency regime, and specifically the Court of Justice decisions in the PTC Therapeutics and MSD Animal Health litigation; both of these cases have been decided since the article was written, and therefore the project will have to backsource the findings directly rather than rely on the summary provided by Kim. Second, does Kim's evidentiary-burden argument under the expropriation standard continue to hold up against the fact pattern presented in Vanda Pharmaceuticals v. FDA, which represents the closest post-2016 U.S. analog to Kim's prediction about the type of fact pattern that would fail under the evidentiary-burden approach, but with an added jurisdictional dimension she did not consider. Third, and most importantly for Dr. Benavides, how should the project resolve the differing interpretations offered by Kim and Ferguson as to the same treaty architecture, and in particular whether the research project should take Kim's politically practical interpretation, Ferguson's doctrinal optimism, or a third option in which Ferguson's pathway is taken as a legitimate interpretive path for purposes of legal reasoning while Kim's proportionality backstop remains as the controlling constraint in practice.
