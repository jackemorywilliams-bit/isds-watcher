### 2B. Hela Schwarz v. China — Article 9(c) Provision Text Recovered (New Sourced Insight)

**Academic intake implementation:** Targeted searches for JIDS and ICSID Review case notes on Hela Schwarz GmbH v. China (ICSID ARB/17/19) were conducted. The 2020 JIDS article — "Parallel Proceedings under Chinese BITs: The Case of Hela Schwarz GmbH v PR China" (Journal of International Dispute Settlement, vol. 11(2), p. 335) — was identified and a vLex UK entry for the article surfaced. Both the Oxford Academic JIDS page and the vLex article body were 403-blocked. However, search-engine snippets of the vLex entry returned material that appears to quote or closely summarize the BIT Protocol provision at issue.

**Article 9(c) text reconstructed:** The search-engine snippet reveals the following text of Article 9(c) of the Protocol to the China-Germany BIT (2003):

> *"in case the issue has been brought to a Chinese court, it can be withdrawn by the investor according to Chinese law."*

This provision text, recovered from the search-engine snippet of the vLex / JIDS 2020 academic article, confirms:

1. **Sub-provision Article 9(c) confirmed at MEDIUM-HIGH confidence** — upgraded from LOWER confidence (single secondary source, July 9). The JIDS article is a peer-reviewed academic publication in a leading ISDS-specific journal, providing a more authoritative source than the general secondary sources from July 9.

2. **No-U-turn label confirmed from provision text** — the provision explicitly contemplates withdrawal ("it can be withdrawn by the investor"). This is the definitional test for a no-U-turn clause vs. a pure fork-in-the-road clause: the investor can cure the defect by withdrawing from domestic proceedings before a judgment, rather than the election being irrevocable from the moment of domestic filing. The JIDS academic literature characterizing Chinese BIT concurrent-proceedings provisions as "no-U-turn clauses" is now corroborated by the provision text itself.

3. **Cure mechanism identified:** Withdrawal "according to Chinese law" — the investor must follow Chinese civil procedure rules to effect the withdrawal. The precise procedural requirements under Chinese law are not addressed in the available snippet.

4. **Hela Schwarz's counter-argument documented:** The claimant argued that Article 9(c) was "irrelevant, given that no domestic proceedings were pending at the time it filed the request for arbitration." This is doctrinally significant: if correct, the Chinese subsidiary's domestic proceedings had concluded (rather than remaining pending) before the ICSID filing — which raises the question of how the tribunal applied Article 9(c). The "has been brought" language (past tense) in the provision could capture completed proceedings as well as pending ones. The tribunal nonetheless upheld the Article 9(c) jurisdictional defense and dismissed the case (Award, December 10, 2025). Without the Award text, the tribunal's actual reasoning on this point is unconfirmed.

**Note:** The JIDS 2020 article predates the December 2025 Award. It documents the concurrent-proceedings dispute as it was framed during the interim phase. The provision text quoted appears to derive from the BIT Protocol itself or from ICSID filings in the case; it is the most authoritative reconstruction of Article 9(c) text in the project record. No post-Award JIDS or ICSID Review case note was found. Sources: [(vLex UK, "Parallel Proceedings under Chinese BITs," citing JIDS 2020)](https://vlex.co.uk/vid/parallel-proceedings-under-chinese-1087449646) [body 403-blocked; text from search-engine snippet]; [(JIDS, Oxford Academic)](https://academic.oup.com/jids/article-abstract/11/2/335/5807886) [abstract only]; [(Jusmundi, concurrent proceedings analysis)](https://jusmundi.com/en/document/publication/en-an-analysis-of-concurrent-proceedings-under-chinese-bits) [body 403-blocked].
