The most authoritative secondary analysis — Steffen Hindelang's piece on the Kluwer Arbitration Blog and the CELIS Institute note — frames the potential claim squarely around control-of-investment and treatment standards, not IP-as-investment. On the substantive footing, the dispute is likely to focus primarily on fair and equitable treatment, but even in that respect state liability is not self-evident. The operative measures are governmental/judicial control instruments: on 30 September 2025 the Dutch Minister of Economic Affairs invoked the Goods Availability Act, and judicial review of the ministerial order is possible but does not suspend the order or other measures, such as those imposed by the Dutch Enterprise Chamber. Shortly after the Dutch intervention, China imposed a company-specific export control measure on all of Nexperia's sites in China.

The one "IP" signal — a report that the ministerial order froze Nexperia's assets, intellectual property, operations, and personnel changes for one year — is the freezing of the company's IP *as part of a total asset/control freeze*, not a characterization of the measure as an IP expropriation for treaty purposes. That is a Ring-1 near-miss, not a hit: IP appears as a frozen corporate asset, not as the asserted covered investment. **The thread's default assumption (no IP dimension) holds; primary sources have not displaced it.** Note also a claimant-side headwind on the merits: the bar for direct or indirect expropriation is high, and the Enterprise Chamber imposed additional measures almost immediately after the government intervention, allowing the argument that damages stem from those measures — an intervening-cause problem, not an IP question.

**Bearing on the research question:** Nexperia touches Ring 2 (a regulatory + judicial measure) cleanly but fails Ring 1 (the asserted investment is shares/control, not IP) and has not reached Ring 3 (no filing, so no jurisdictional disposal). It remains outside our intersection.

## Non-retroactivity thread (secondary pivot)

Because Wingtech yielded nothing by mid-week on the IP framing, I advanced the non-retroactivity thread and surfaced a distinction worth recording. The "manifest lack of merit" gate is now being codified beyond ICSID: the 2026 ICC Rules, Article 30, expressly provide the power in the body of the Rules and define the grounds — manifest lack of merit or manifest lack of jurisdiction over claims or defences — while preserving tribunal discretion in shaping the procedure. This sits alongside the ICSID track, where a party may object that a claim is manifestly without legal merit and request an expedited procedure at the preliminary stage under Additional Facility Rule 51(1).

**Insight (new to the record):** this "manifest lack of merit" mechanism is analytically *distinct* from the abuse-of-right / critical-date admissibility doctrine on which our seed cases (esp. Philip Morris v Australia) turn. The manifest-lack-of-merit gate targets the legal futility of a claim; the abuse-of-right gate targets the jurisdictional propriety of post-dispute restructuring. For a future IP-as-investment claim under a pre-existing BIT, disposal would still most plausibly run through the discretionary abuse-of-right/foreseeable-dispute route, not the codified merit gate — so a researcher tracking doctrinal shift should not read the spread of manifest-lack-of-merit rules as evidence that IP-as-investment claims are being gated differently. The two must not be conflated.
