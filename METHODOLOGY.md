## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo sets out the operational methodology of the ISDS Thematic Watcher — an automated monitoring tool I built for this project using Claude AI and Claude Code as the development medium. Every operational choice in its development was grounded in the literature reviewed to date. Beyond documenting the methodology, the memo speaks to one of the project's substantive questions: How can changes in investor-State dispute settlement practice be identified, over time and within a particular area of law, where an intellectual property right is asserted as a covered investment, a governmental act or court decision is challenged as a violation of that right, and a rule of law applied at the jurisdictional or admissibility level determines that the case cannot proceed — and how can that be done repeatedly and uniformly? Rather than resolve the substance of those issues — whether trade secrets and clinical trial data qualify as protected investments — this memo addresses the methodological approach used to identify the areas of law that meet those criteria; for the substantive arguments on what rights are entitled to protection under international investment agreements, the Ferguson and Kim memos should be consulted. What follows is how the ISDS Thematic Watcher turns that legal framework into a repeatable process with multiple points of auditability, including contestation where necessary. The memo proceeds through the theoretical basis of the instrument (Part II), its source architecture and sourcing mechanism (Part III), candidate identification and enrichment (Part IV), a two-stage classification cascade (Part V), scoring, calibration, and validation (Part VI), the reporting standard for the digest (Part VII), reproducibility (Part VIII), and a candid account of limitations (Part IX).


## II. Theoretical Framework — The Three-Ring Thematic Fingerprint

### A. Why a thematic instrument, and not a keyword search
What I am watching for is not one specific word or phrase. A relationship among three doctrines cannot be reduced to one keyword; each time you try, you lose the essence of the idea. In one case a court may reference the term “patent” a hundred times in the briefing and yet have no relation to my claim; conversely, a court may never once say “abuse of right,” and the case still fits the category exactly. So as much as this looked like a lexical problem, it was really an interpretive one, and that is where qualitative method comes in: how do you develop a tool to identify a category when there are only three examples to work with? Treating the fingerprint's development as an exercise in theory-informed sampling and most-similar-case design — which Linos & Carlson urge on legal scholars, since legal authors tend to pick examples unsystematically and analyze them in purely doctrinal terms (Linos & Carlson, Qualitative Methods for Law Review Writing, 84 U. Chi. L. Rev. 213 (2017)) — I did not build the seed corpus to represent all of ISDS. I built the seed corpus out of structural similarity along the dimensions I care about — the logic of a most-similar-case method.

### B. The seed corpus
The three awards selected represent the three forms of arbitration awards that have the same type of structure in all three subject matter areas. Each award has the structural elements outlined by the project for identifying those structural elements. In Philip Morris Asia Ltd. v. Commonwealth of Australia, PCA Case No. 2012-12, Award on Jurisdiction and Admissibility (Dec. 17, 2015), Philip Morris made a claim against an Australian statute which required tobacco products to be sold with blank packaging claiming its treaty based Intellectual Property Protections. The Arbitral Tribunal denied the Claim as an Abuse of Right because Philip Morris had created a new entity in Hong Kong so that it could obtain Treaty Rights in regard to a foreseen dispute. In Eli Lilly & Company v. Government of Canada, ICSID Case No. UNCT/14/2, Final Award (March 16, 2017), Eli Lilly challenged the Courts of Canada's decision to invalidate Eli Lilly's patents using the "Promise Utility Doctrine", which was a judicial tool used to invalidate the patents and Eli Lilly claimed that the invalidations violated Article 1105 of NAFTA's Minimum Standard of Treatment, which defined Denial of Justice. Unlike Philip Morris, this case was decided on the merits. The Arbitral Tribunal determined there was no drastic change in Canadian Patent Law and did not find anything arbitrary or discriminatory in the utility requirement (Eli Lilly & Co. v. Canada, Final Award (Mar. 16, 2017), italaw case 1625, https://www.italaw.com/cases/1625; IISD, Investment Treaty News (June 12, 2017), https://www.iisd.org/itn/2017/06/12/highly-anticipated-nafta-award-rejects-patent-law-related-claim-against-canada-matthew-levine/). In Bridgestone Licensing Services, Inc. & Bridgestone Americas, Inc. v. Republic of Panama, ICSID Case No. ARB/16/34, Award (August 14, 2020), Bridgestone claimed that a Judgment entered by the Supreme Court of Panama constituted a Denial of Justice. It also raised the operating entity versus holding company question, i.e. whether the Trademark Licenses were a Covered Investment -- jurisdictional/standing issues similar to the Corporate Structure Issues involved in Philip Morris Asia Ltd. However, the Arbitral Tribunal primarily ruled in favor of the Claimants regarding that threshold question during the Expedited Objections phase and thereafter denied both the Denial of Justice and Fair and Equitable Treatment Claims on the Merits concluding there was no Denial of Justice and no demonstrated harm (Bridgestone v. Panama, Award (Aug. 14, 2020), italaw case 4475, https://www.italaw.com/cases/4475; Decision on Expedited Objections (Dec. 13, 2017), https://jusmundi.com/en/document/decision/en-bridgestone-americas-inc-and-bridgestone-licensing-services-inc-v-republic-of-panama-decision-on-expedited-objections-wednesday-13th-december-2017). The Index Unit for all awards is the Arbitrators' language itself, i.e. the operational phrases used were directly extracted from each award as shown by the full Per-Ring Extraction in PLAN.md. This selection was intentional and corresponds to one of the basic principles of information retrieval: The best terms for distinguishing among relevant documents will always be those appearing within those documents themselves (Salton & McGill, Introduction to Modern Information Retrieval (1983); Manning et al., Introduction to Information Retrieval (2008)).

The two basic concepts of the literature review -- the memos from Ferguson and Kim can be seen in their full form at lit-review/ -- were the basis of the research question and will serve as the base for Ring 1; however, they did not represent a fourth case, rather they complemented the three arbitration awards. In Trade Secrets at Risk, Alexander Ferguson stated that trade secrets are likely to be the most vulnerable category of intellectual property and further stated that there already exists an adequate amount of protections for trade secrets as covered investments under existing regimes when the regime is properly interpreted: he defines the asset through TRIPS Article 39 and VCLT Article 31(3)(c) and states that this protection is threatened by a four part risk taxonomy (disclosure, use, regulation, and combinations of these), and also provides a four-question indirect expropriation test -- does the state's protection have to be absolute? Has the state caused a significant interference with the legal protection of the secret, with the secret's exclusive commercial use, or a significant decrease in the commercial value of the secret? All of this is based on a recognition/enforceability gap which represents a structural issue: International law only protects that which municipal law of the host state currently recognizes. The direct counterpoint is Daria Kim’s Protecting Trade Secrets under International Investment Law: reasoning from pharmaceutical clinical trial data, she concludes that such data is unlikely to win protection through ISDS. She has four reasons for this conclusion. Clinical data is built differently from other IP assets, and that difference shows up right at the “investment” threshold. The Apotex v. United States decision marks the outer limit of what constitutes an “investment” — the point past which protection runs out — while Philip Morris v. Uruguay supplies a discretionary jurisdictional backstop through the Salini contribution-to-development criterion. Whenever the disclosure is made in the form of non-commercial regulation, it is often difficult or even impossible to establish the counterfactual of what would have occurred had the data remained confidential; this creates a substantial evidentiary burden for the party seeking compensation under the expropriation standard. The legitimate-expectations approach via fair and equitable treatment is so vague as to provide no basis for implying confidentiality. Finally, TRIPS Article 39(3) establishes a requirement to protect against unfair commercial use, but it does not prevent a government from disclosing the data generally; because of this, a disclosure made for public-health purposes may be viewed as non-compensable under the proportionality and police-powers carve-out. The real difference between Ferguson and Kim — his optimism set against her structural skepticism about the very same treaty architecture (BITs, the ICSID Convention, TRIPS Article 39, and the VCLT) — is the core of the conceptual framework for the research question. And Ring 1 takes its working vocabulary (trade secret, clinical trial data, test data, data exclusivity, unfair commercial use, TRIPS Article 39(3)) from these two memoranda rather than from any individual award, along with the doctrinal stakes that justify surfacing a development at all.

### C. The three rings
The first ring — intellectual property as a protected investment. The most significant recurring theme across the readings has been whether, and under what conditions, intellectual property can be considered a “covered investment” (Mercurio, Awakening the Sleeping Giant, 15 J. Int'l Econ. L. 871 (2012); Correa & Viñuales, Intellectual Property Rights as Protected Investments: How Open are the Gates?, 19 J. Int'l Econ. L. 91 (2016)). The first ring is at its strongest where the asset is information based, where ownership is divided between a holding company and an operating company (as it was in Bridgestone), and where it shares the structural vulnerability that the Ferguson and Kim memos identified for trade secrets and clinical trial data. This ring also incorporates the Salini criteria of contribution, duration, risk, and contribution to the host economy, and those criteria hand tribunals considerable discretion in applying them (compare Salini Costruttori S.p.A. v. Morocco, ICSID Case No. ARB/00/4). Triggers for ring one include: covered investment; promise utility doctrine; exploitation of the trademark.
Ring Two – Regulatory/Judicial Action as the Challenged Behavior (Weighted). Two of the three seeds were brought against domestic courts rather than government agency action, and both alleged a violation of procedural due process resulting in a denial of justice. The standards for holding a state liable for the actions of its judiciary come from Paulsson, Denial of Justice in International Law (2005): a State can only be held liable for its courts' decisions upon a clear demonstration of manifest injustice, and mere error does not meet that standard. The seed tribunals use essentially the same terms to articulate their findings of liability — conduct that was outrageous and egregious, manifestly unjust judgments, rulings so erroneous that no reasonable or competent tribunal could have rendered them. Because the seed corpus relies so heavily on this ring, the weight I applied to it is commensurately heavy — a claimant alleging a wrongly rendered court decision may achieve MEDIUM on this ring alone. My weighting of the judicial-measure ring in this fashion was deliberate — a design-phase decision, not an oversight. There is admittedly some risk in it, since an IP-as-investment case reaching the threshold through an administrative route alone would likely be under-read. This ring, however, will only ever reach MEDIUM on its own, and never HIGH; so while the weighting widens recall, no single doctrine can manufacture a top-band hit unaided, and the blind spot that remains is stated plainly in Part IX. In addition to these acknowledged methods of determining categories' weights based on frequency of claims within a coding corpus (Saldana, Coding Manual), there are several other recognized methods. Triggers for ring two include: denial of justice; manifestly unjust judgment; minimum standard of treatment.
Ring Three – Threshold Questions regarding Jurisdiction and Admissibility as a Potentially Dispositive Dimension. This ring observes the ground on which a case may be denied disposition prior to having its merits addressed: abuse of right, treaty shopping, foreseeability of the dispute, restructuring for treaty protection, and the disputed definitions of "the investor" and/or "the investment." Among the seeds, this dimension was primarily established through Philip Morris v. Australia — the only case that was decided based on it (held inadmissible as an abuse of right without addressing the merits). It played a secondary role in Bridgestone where the standing/covered investment question was answered mostly for the claimants, and thus the merits of the case determined the outcome; and it did not form part of the reasoning in Eli Lilly — which was decided on the merits. The point here is that these preliminary questions may act as dispositive issues — and did in Philip Morris — even though all three seeds were not decided based on these issues. The most recent authority used to develop the integration is Baumgartner, Treaty Shopping in International Investment Law (2016) — which expands upon an earlier abuse-of-right principle (Byers, Abuse of Rights: An Old Principle, A New Age, 47 McGill L.J. 389 (2002)) — alongside the standards provided in the leading texts (Dolzer & Schreuer, Principles of International Investment Law (2nd ed. 2012); McLachlan et al., International Investment Arbitration: Substantive Principles (2nd ed. 2017)). Triggers for ring three include: abuse of right; reasonably foreseeable; critical date; shell subsidiary.

### D. The scoring grammar
The scoring grammar defines when a band is considered crossed. For a HIGH score (70+), two rings need to be visible. A MEDIUM score (40-69) can be supported by either a strong first ring paired with a weak second ring, or by the judicial measure on its own. A LOW score (< 40) means a single weak ring or no rings at all. For extractive disputes, sovereign debt disputes, and intra-EU energy disputes, an automatic LOW is applied unless there is some evidence of an IP or judicial angle. All phrases and their corresponding weights, summing to 100 per ring, reside in machine-readable format in fingerprint.yaml. Each phrase is searched for within all three fields of an item — title, summary, and body text — as a case-insensitive substring instead of a whole word, so the trigger fires regardless of surrounding words. The fingerprint also captures variants of terms that inflect heavily via truncated stems such as expropriat-, however it does not capture variants via lemmatization or regular-expression pattern matching; this too is recorded in Part IX as a known simplification.


## III. Source Architecture & Research Methods
I chose a methodical approach when selecting the source materials and testing their credibility, and I strategically selected multiple different kinds of sources. Doing so let me get a wide array of information concerning investment disputes, structured around the four ways I could find such information: institutional documents, archival records, analyses, and practitioner commentary. The open repositories I identified were the ICSID docket; UNCTAD's Investment Dispute Settlement Navigator and World Investment Report; italaw's primary-document archive; IISD's Investment Treaty News; and IAReporter, limited to headline level. To acquire more than the open repositories offer, I added two further channels. The first gives access to the operator's own Google Alerts feeds, polled as RSS, which provide updates on both pending and seed case developments. The second acquires new academic literature related to the seed cases and doctrines through Google Scholar alerts, received in a designated mailbox. The amount of material accessible splits into three categories. Full read: I can view all content on the ICSID docket, UNCTAD, italaw, IISD Investment Treaty News, and the operator's own Google Alerts and Google Scholar feeds, as the publishers allow. Headline-only read: IAReporter, which publishes only titles and leads because it keeps article bodies behind a paywall. Disabled: Google News RSS, currently blocked by that site's robots.txt file, together with any individual page denied by a robots.txt rule or a login wall. This tiered access is why I view the tool as a lead-generation floor and nothing more: the minimum count of meaningful events that publicly available text can produce, never a total census of ISDS activity at the intersection. The primary motivation behind the design was triangulation — cross-checking between separate collections — on the assumption that results appearing the same way in two or more independent sources are more likely to reflect an actual pattern than a random occurrence. The repositories used by the tool are also the ones I use manually when back-sourcing; the tool just works across all of them simultaneously instead of one at a time. Collection follows predetermined extraction criteria: once relevant listings have been identified, the tool retrieves the original full-text document of the proceeding and records a reference for each item, along with the access date and time. That unique identifier is also how duplication is avoided; because the seen-state file at state/seen.json keys each processed item by its source and id, the same listing is never evaluated twice. Listings referencing the same dispute in multiple feeds are then resolved downstream, at the Research Brief's deduplicated insight ledger, rather than during intake; fully merging one dispute across feeds at the scoring stage — by matching party names, dates, and case numbers — is not yet done, and remains a recognized limitation described in Part IX. On privacy and access: I have never pulled information from behind any subscription paywall or authentication barrier; I follow standard robot exclusion protocols, never make requests to the same domain at intervals shorter than three seconds, and include identifying contact information in the User-Agent header. The one possible exception is the operator's own subscribed Google Alerts feeds, which Google provides for the operator to poll within a feed reader.


## IV. Candidate Identification & Enrichment
Since a candidate's content determines how it gets identified and grouped, enrichment has to happen before classification. To enrich, I first obtain the candidate's source document, strip out the non-readable content, and assess each remaining sentence for its doctrinal density. That assessment counts the fingerprint keywords contained in the sentence (these carry the highest weight), then the holding verbs (held, found, dismissed), then the evaluative terms (egregious, manifest, denial); sentences composed of little but dates, or written entirely in capitals, are excluded, so that paragraph numbers and case captions do not receive high scores. The classifier then scores the readable body, and the sentence with the highest score becomes the citable line for the candidate. In much the same way that a properly placed pin citation lets a researcher immediately find the referenced passage from a well-researched bibliography via back-sourcing, that line gives the reader at least one sentence worth reading and gives the digest a verbatim quote to serve as an anchor.


## V. Classification Procedure — A Two-Stage Cascade
There are two stages to classification, and each has its own advantages and drawbacks for precision and recall. Stage one is lexical coding – deterministic, and transparent. All candidates are ranked based on the keyword weights contained within the fingerprint.yaml file using a classical weighted term matching strategy (Salton & McGill, 1983). Enrichment will then be run on the highest-ranked candidate(s) from this stage. Lexical Coding can occur with or without a Language Model therefore even when no LM is installed, an ordered, inspectable result will be returned along with a set of scores. Stage two is contextual classification – paraphrase tolerant. Only the top ranked candidate(s) from Stage one will be fed into a Language Model (either Anthropic's Claude, or Google's Gemini, determined at runtime) via a few shot prompt that includes all relevant ring definitions, examples of high, medium, low exemplars, and negatives. The model is learning the boundaries of what constitutes a match rather than simply identifying the target (few shot prompts improve task adherence without fine tuning: Brown et al., "Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners", NeurIPS 33 (2020)). The model MUST produce a valid JSON string. A valid JSON string carries a relevance score, the number of rings matched, thematic tags, a two-sentence summary annotation of the results, and a verbatim citable line. Any malformed output will trigger ONE additional round of stricter retries. If the model fails again after this single round of retries it will log classification_failed. Quota errors and/or network errors will cause the instrument to fall back to the lexical score therefore some sort of verdict will ALWAYS be produced. If the system does fall back, the item is flagged as such via a tag (keyword_fallback or classification_error_fallback), and the digest header reports the classifier as keyword-fallback rather than naming the model; readers can therefore always distinguish an LLM-written annotation from a purely lexical one. Because a language model can paraphrase a sentence and still present it as a quotation, the verbatim citable line is accepted only when it appears, character for character after whitespace and quotation marks are normalized, within the source text; a line that fails this check is discarded and the deterministic extractor supplies the quote instead. The few-shot prompt and this substring verification together bound the model's output, but they do not remove the need for human-in-the-loop review, and the digest annotations are meant to be verified by a reader before they are relied upon.


## VI. Scoring, Calibration & Validation

### A. Calibration decisions
A ring cannot be considered present until it has earned the right to be. It only counts once its lexical subtotal reaches a floor of twelve — enough to require a real hit on one or more terms rather than a brush against the surface of one. The reason for the minimum of twelve was the desire to remove false positives, as when a mining claim merely mentions an investment and gets confused for the theme; that minimum term-frequency cut-off (Manning et al. 2008) was intentional. The digest threshold of forty, meanwhile, is twenty less than the original figure. Neither value was picked arbitrarily; both come from the natural distribution the deterministic scorer generates. When both on-theme and off-theme text were fed through the system, the cases fell into two distinctly different groups separated by a significant gap: labelled off-theme texts scored between zero and eight, with a mean of roughly one, while labelled on-theme texts scored between forty and seventy-five. No case scored in the range of nine to thirty-nine. The forty therefore sits at the lower end of the on-theme cluster, and the twelve sits in the dead band immediately above the off-theme noise. Both values were selected from direct observation, as opposed to intuition.

The instrument does not exercise judgment when it sends out results. It simply reports leads related to what was searched. Because the topics are narrow, many weeks will have very little content, and a reader could become conditioned to stop reviewing the digests after an extended stretch of near-empty weeks. To prevent that, the instrument is governed by a floor rather than dumping everything available. Any item scoring forty or higher on the scale is always reported to the user, and no ceiling exists on how many may be reported. In a week with fewer than six matches, the digest is filled toward six with the next-best candidates; no candidate scoring below twenty-five will ever be presented. Candidates brought forward this way are called watch-list leads, not matches — a user should never equate a lead with a holding. And if a week passes in which no candidate meets the floor for relevance, the report says so plainly, along with the number of candidates evaluated against the topic.

### B. Validation
The lexical scorer was tested at two levels, and neither level amounts to strong validation. Level one was a face-validity test against the seed examples in fingerprint.yaml. The scorer classified the seven labelled examples correctly on their own text, along with the three negative examples. That indicates the scorer can recognize the corpus that generated it. Level two asked whether the scorer had simply memorized the three positive seeds or had generalized a pattern, so I ran an exploratory holdout of twenty items using eval_holdout.py. Four of the twenty were on-theme positives — awards that took no part in the development process, namely Philip Morris v. Uruguay, Mondev v. United States, Loewen v. United States, and Apotex v. United States — and they were compared against sixteen other awards randomly pulled from the original data. Evaluating at a score of forty, the scorer yielded a precision of 1.00, a recall of 0.75, and an accuracy of 0.95, for an F1 of 0.86; none of the off-theme awards was classified positively, and three of the four unknown on-theme awards were. Given there were only four total positives in this evaluation, care is warranted in any claim about overall performance; the F1 of 0.86 in particular is illustrative rather than conclusive. That three of four unknown classifications registered at all suggests the fingerprint has some ability to generalize beyond its development corpus, but with a sample this small, that is little more than a suggestion.


## VII. The Reporting Standard — The Digest as Annotated Bibliography
I selected the annotated bibliography as the digest format because the reporting standard called for interpretive description of the law, as opposed to simply summarizing it. Each entry accordingly contains both a citation and a descriptive-and-evaluative annotation. Entries generally run 50-200 words per the AALL Law Library Journal guidelines, carry references in the style of The Chicago Manual of Style, and provide a URL so readers can locate the source. Every entry assesses the relevance of the item to the overall project and, if relevant, how. Each links back to the originating document and includes at least one direct verbatim quote from it, giving the reader enough to find the source without additional searching. And any limitation on accessing the original — paywall, login, metadata-only — is clearly noted, never concealed.

### The Interpretive Companion — The Research Council & the Research Brief
In addition to the digest, the instrument generates an accompanying interpretive document in the same cycle: the ISDS Research Brief. The Brief is produced by a small council of role-bound stages, each trained to its task through a dedicated instruction prompt or a deterministic check, and the stages are determined by the roles filling them. The chairman sets the weekly agenda using forward threads. The analyst evaluates the screened developments relative to the research question, proposing each substantive claim as a structured candidate. Every proposed claim must then pass a deterministic integrity gate that verifies it, by exact identifier, against my append-only verification ledger; in parallel, a deterministic citation check machine-verifies each cited source; and an editor creates the final product. While that final document is coming together, the analyst may conduct web searches to deepen insight on developments already screened — but web search cannot be used as a second collection channel. Each of these prompted stages works under a binding calibration checklist that is meant to catch the failures models are most known for: made-up citations or quotes, claims that reach past the evidence provided, and relevance that gets overstated. Any high-risk element; citations, quotes, holdings, dates, figures; has to be checked against its real source before it can be asserted, or else it gets marked unavailable instead. That pre-publication check is the anti-hallucination workflow, and the deterministic ledger gate sits in front as gatekeeper while the citation check backs it up. There is also an analytics piece that watches which catalogue sources have actually been responsive to the thematic intersection, and through this means the coverage can be adapted over time based on which feeds are actually producing content. The full council meets every day. Its weekly reconvening results in two documents; the brief and the chairman's candid accountability assessment. As such, the analysis does not start over when a day ends; instead the analyst keeps a living state-of-the-answer synthesis of what is currently known, plus an append-only, deduplicated insight ledger where each session gets compared against the knowledge that existed before it. Every so often, a recurring human-review checkpoint will spot-audit a sample of the claims that were cited over the period, and the outcomes get logged. The brief, the deliberation of the council, and the accountability record are all archived together with each digest.


## VIII. Reproducibility & State
All data passing through the instrument is saved to a state file, state/seen.json, keyed by source and a unique id, which means every item is processed once and never again. Even if the state file becomes corrupted beyond recovery, the pipeline restarts rather than stopping. Each scheduled run is also stored in a version-controlled repository, along with a copy of every digest by date and a snapshot of the current state — all items collected and their scores — providing essentially the same documentation that manual annotation of original observations would.


## IX. Limitations
There are limitations of coverage and access for this project and I make them clear at the outset, so the reader will know just how much of the field the tool can see. In terms of construct validity, the theme was developed from three awards, so the range of doctrinal variants those seeds produce may be an underestimate. Theme-extension terms carry low weights so that adjacent doctrines like Denial of Benefits and Substantial Business Activity remain reachable by the tool, but that lessens the limit rather than removing it. A most-similar-case method built on three seeds also invites the argument that the results reflect overfitting. The small exploratory hold-out set in Part VI.B — twenty items, only four positives — represents at least a partial answer, since three of the four unseen awards were correctly identified. The remainder is a legitimate concern: four confirmatory cases can never exhaust all the language possibilities the instrument's structure must handle. Furthermore, the hold out set is likely skewed toward NAFTA and ICSID denial-of-justice awards (Loewen and Mondev utilize more of the judicial measure ring than the full IP-as-investment intersection), and thus Ring 1 is utilized substantially less independently than Rings 2 and 3, so a pure IP-as-investment case with no judicial measure component would represent the instrument's most likely false negative. No claim beyond face-plus-holdout validity can be made until the scorer is validated against a much larger human-coded sample of non-English and non-NAFTA awards. Two implementation simplifications belong in this register for the same reason. The lexical matcher uses case-insensitive substrings and a limited set of truncated stems instead of every possible lemma in a dictionary, so an unusual form of a trigger term may avoid being recognized. And since duplicates are eliminated per source using a stable id, a dispute appearing via multiple feeds is resolved downstream at the Research Brief's insight ledger rather than being merged during intake. Sources that produce only headlines — IAReporter among them — often de-rate the most genuinely on-theme cases, where the decisive information sits inside a paywalled body the tool cannot access; those items appear as watch list leads rather than matches. Coverage is a floor. Paywalls and robots.txt can withhold content, and with Google News RSS blocked and inactive for now, the instrument shows the least that can exist — not a complete census.  The Research Brief presents an additional risk that is distinct from the ones above: it utilizes model generated interpretations and, through bounded web searches, material outside of the curated corpus, both of which can create citation hallucinations or exaggerate holdings — reduced not removed by the council's verification process and the deliberate constraint placed on searching — so the interpretations and supplemental findings contained within the Research Brief are provided as research leads that need to be validated prior to their utilization and not as conclusions.

A further limitation concerns the verification status of the council's interpretive output. Since July 2026 the Research Brief may assert a finding only if the operator has personally verified the underlying claim in the append-only verification ledger (an exact-identifier lookup; a reachable source URL is never treated as evidence the claim is true); everything not so verified is presented as an unverified lead or routed for library access. This restricts what the brief can state plainly, and it means the assertable record grows no faster than my own capacity to review — a trade of coverage for reliability that I made deliberately. Additionally, a re-weighting of the Ring 1 lexicon toward the trade-secret and clinical-data vocabulary was approved and applied in July 2026, raising that cluster to the dominant weight in the ring while preserving sensitivity to the patent and trademark case shapes; the approval and its terms are recorded in working/FINGERPRINT_DRIFT.md, which also notes that the weighting is to be revisited upward once a decided trade-secret ISDS case converts the hypothesized pathway into case law.
