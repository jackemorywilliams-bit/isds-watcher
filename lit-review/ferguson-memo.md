## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo analyzes Ferguson's article, as it relates to the broader framing issue that has guided this part of the literature review. In what ways are trade secrets protected as "investments" (under international investment law and investor-State arbitration), and what specific legal protections do investors have against a host state that threatens to disclose, regulate, or use their trade secret? The question was written with the intention of being split into two. The first part of the question is essentially a definitional and jurisdictional question about whether a trade secret would meet the definition of an "investment" and therefore qualify for ISDS protection at all. The second portion of the question is much more substantive and focuses on which doctrines exist to protect an investor after they have qualified for jurisdictional protection from the host state, but the host state has already disclosed, used, or regulated the secret. There are very few pieces in the current body of literature that consider both parts of the question separately; thus, I felt it necessary to create a dedicated memo for Ferguson's piece within a multifaceted research question forum.

## II. Source Overview
### A. Argument and Framework
The primary basis of Mr. Ferguson's position is that the risks to a trade secret are more likely to materialize than the risks to other intellectual property rights — a State, as he puts it, is more likely to disclose a trade secret than exploit a trademark — and that trade secrets therefore require protection as "Investments" within modern Bilateral Investment Treaties (BITs) and International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes ("ISDS"), as well as the doctrine of indirect expropriation without compensation — which will serve as the best method to protect against the risks posed by an action of a State towards a foreign investor's Secret. Ferguson puts three forms of State conduct in focus — disclosure, use, and regulation — and it is only after the police-powers filter is applied that he arrives at the four risks named in his conclusion: disclosure without a lawful basis that does not pursue a legitimate welfare objective; disclosure that is unlawful in the host State; use by the State for a commercial purpose as a market actor; and regulation for a purpose that is not a legitimate public welfare objective. The "four risks" of his abstract are therefore the residue of his whole argument, not its opening classification. Ferguson develops this position using two analytical steps. First, he outlines a process of defining what constitutes an "investment," starting with the reference to Article 39 of the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS), continuing to define the trade secret as recognized at the local level, and finally concluding with how it defines "investment" in the context of BITs, while using Article 31(3)(c) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) to incorporate TRIPS as an additional rule of international law binding upon the Parties. Second, Mr. Ferguson outlines a four-part inquiry to determine whether an action of a State towards a foreign investor's trade secret is considered an act of indirect expropriation. The four inquiries ask: (1) Is the state protection absolute? (2) Has there been significant interference in the legal protection afforded to the trade secret? (3) Has there been significant interference in the exclusive commercial use of the trade secret? (4) Has there been a substantial reduction in the commercial value of the trade secret?

## IV. Substantive Analysis
### A. Risk Architecture
The threat landscape facing a foreign investor's trade secret can be organized by Ferguson into three forms of State conduct: disclosure, use, and regulation. He states them plainly — "Three issues are in focus: the disclosure, use, or regulation of a trade secret" — and the four risks named in his conclusion are what remains of those three once the police-powers filter is applied, not a separate opening classification. The classification method used by Ferguson is atypical because it is based on function (what the State actually did) rather than on procedure or evidence (both of which would arise later in the analysis), and it is ordered according to how the State engages with the secret. Disclosure captures how the State exposes the secret to the public through its apparatus for transparency. Use becomes relevant when the State, or a State-owned entity, turns a trade secret to commercial gain against the investor who holds it. Ferguson gives two examples. In Tethyan Copper a provincial government is said to have built its own mining project on the strength of the claimant's feasibility studies. In OI European Group v. Venezuela a State-owned enterprise took over Dutch-held glass plants, and the tribunal found that Venezuela had expropriated the plants together with the technology and know-how used in them. Saluka belongs to his disclosure category rather than this one: there the Czech Government leaked a bank's financial position to the media, and the tribunal found a breach of the non-impairment obligation. Regulation becomes relevant when the State requires the owner of the secret to provide information about the secret so that they can have access to a regulated market, and this is where Ferguson identifies the pharmaceuticals and tobacco ingredient archetypes. What makes Ferguson's classification method useful for this research project is his recognition that trade secrets are particularly vulnerable to normal State activity compared to all other intellectual property rights. As Ferguson explains, a State is much more likely to expose a trade secret during its normal activities than it is to exploit a trademark; thus, threats to trade secrets are both more numerous and more likely to occur than threats to all other categories of intellectual property.

### B. Recognition / Enforceability Gap
There is another structural risk in addition to those, and that is the difference in how the host state recognizes the secrecy of the investment and how it can be enforced via international law. Ferguson's argument is that international law doesn't generate trade secret protections for investments; instead, international law protects those areas in an investment that are already recognized by the host state's municipal laws. Thus, when a trade secret investor does not have legal protection of their trade secret in the host state, they do not have an "investment" to protect, even though the exact same trade secret could have been protected and valued in the investor's own country. Therefore, from a double-contingency perspective, the investor's secret must be recognized and validated by the municipal laws of the host state, and then it must meet the definition of "investment" prior to the substance of the expropriation doctrine ever being engaged with. Ferguson views this aspect of the system as a benefit because it would preserve State sovereignty over the definitional question — though that reading is mine, not his; Ferguson states the requirement and does not evaluate it.

### C. Evidentiary Standards
Ferguson has a very limited discussion of the evidentiary structure; while I believe this is an omission as opposed to a deliberate decision by Ferguson, he does not reach the problem at all. Having now read the article in full, I can say that it contains no discussion of how a claimant proves the existence of a trade secret without destroying it. On the machinery: "in camera" and "protective order" return zero occurrences, but the redaction limb of my earlier sentence was wrong and I withdraw it — his note 182 cites "[t]he heavily redacted award in Les Laboratoires Servier v Poland (Award) (14 February 2012, UNCITRAL) [107]", suggesting it "may have related to misuse of data submitted for regulatory approval for a product." So he does reach redacted publication, once, in a footnote, and does not follow it up with any analysis. My previous version asserted an absolute absence and then, twenty-five words later, said he does not follow up the same three items — two statements that cannot both stand. His apparent unstated position is that the evidentiary machinery of current arbitral practice suffices for the proof-of-recognition phase, and that the harder issues reside within the substantive law rather than the procedural law. This is where Ferguson is weakest, and where the research project will need to seek additional support from outside of the substantive-doctrine literature, most probably in the form of the procedural-rules literature, including Aceris Law's commentary on confidentiality orders (practitioner commentary) and the IBA and ICC guidelines on protecting trade secrets in arbitration.

### D. Pharmaceutical Data and Regulatory Disclosure
Ferguson does engage pharmaceutical test data, but obliquely, and primarily through the tobacco ingredient fact-pattern and through the lens of regulation aimed at achieving a "public welfare" (health) goal. He also does not mention the Vanda Pharmaceuticals litigation against the United States by name. My original sentence said the piece was published before that case began and called the omission unfortunate; a later revision said the reverse and called the omission harder to excuse. Both went further than the record supports, and I am withdrawing the characterisation in either direction. The opinions have since been retrieved and read — *Vanda Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. United States*, No. 23-629C (Fed. Cl.), slip opinions of 18 January 2024 and 22 January 2025 — and the full annotation, with its carrying quotations, is the Kim memo's Vanda entry, which this memo follows rather than duplicates. The dates now sit in one order: the action was commenced on 1 May 2023; on 18 January 2024 the court denied the government's motion to dismiss the takings count and deferred the ownership question; Ferguson's article carries an advance-access date of 14 May 2024 on its own first page, confirmed against the PDF; and on 22 January 2025 the court granted judgment on the pleadings for the government on the ground that the specifications were not trade secrets or protected confidential or proprietary information. So the dispute and the first ruling both predate his publication and only the final disposition follows it. What was available to him at his submission stage I still do not know, and I am not characterising the omission beyond the dates. No reporter page pin is used here — the retrieved documents are slip opinions — and no appellate status is asserted. What the matter contributes to this memo is narrow: a takings claim over regulatory disclosure of pharmaceutical data sits squarely inside his regulation archetype, on facts he does not work through. However, the article does a good job of framing the inherent tension of the issue. In order to achieve its public welfare objectives (such as health), the state may need to force the owner of a trade secret to reveal the secret, and after the information has been revealed, the government agency can then act on that information to enforce regulations without having to compensate the original holder. This process devalues the trade secret, but Ferguson frames it as a legitimate application of police power. Ferguson's implied response to the question of whether ISDS will provide protections to assets whose value is based upon their secrecy is a qualified "yes", i.e., ISDS will protect those assets through the expropriation provisions if Ferguson's four question framework determines that the loss of commercial exclusivity represents a material impact (i.e. a substantial interference); and, if the actions taken by the state cannot be reframed as a genuine attempt to regulate. The qualifier matters because many regulatory schemes (including those concerning disclosure requirements) are specifically intended to appear as though they represent a genuine attempt to regulate; and thus, the amount of actual protection that Ferguson can realistically provide to pharmaceutical investors is narrower than what could potentially be provided under the theory.

### E. Reforms / Position Within the Benavides Project
Ferguson has no plans to revise treaties, amend institutions, nor create additional procedural mechanisms; therefore, his is an interpretative, rather than legislative, project. Furthermore, Ferguson believes the current investment treaty regime, interpreted via the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) and the TRIPS Agreement, contains all the needed doctrinal support to protect trade secrets. In addition, he believes that the four questions he has proposed are merely a restatement of what courts have always done when assessing whether there was expropriatory action, and thus they do not represent calls for new law. If I can identify anything approaching a reform proposal from the article, it is his idea that the analysis within the substantial interference prong of the expropriation test should center around the trade secret itself and not upon the entire scope of the investor's investment. This represents a significant expansion in protections available to investors who possess specialized trade-secret holdings as opposed to those with diversified investments. Another important aspect of this study relates to the fact that Ferguson had identified areas where there were clear gaps in the system, however, he located all solutions solely in the realm of doctrine. Thus, this is ultimately a wager on the ability of arbitrators to be persuaded as much as the research project wishes.

## V. Relationship to Kim
### A. Points of Disagreement with Kim
Ferguson adds to Daria Kim's approach, with respect to the area of operation where Kim is most weak, namely the operationalization of trade secrets within ISDS. He also contests Kim with respect to the area of operation where both scholars have differing prior beliefs concerning the extent to which treaty language may be used to complete the requisite amount of doctrinal development. For example, whereas Kim views the failure of current treaties to provide adequate trade secret protection in ISDS as a structural issue requiring treaty reform, Ferguson views this deficiency as an interpretative problem that is resolvable by tribunals using superior decision-making. This disagreement is genuine and therefore the research project will either need to side with Ferguson or reject his conclusions without being able to "split the baby." I believe at present that Ferguson has the best legal argument regarding what doctrine will permit trade secret protection to be provided via tribunals (i.e., through VCLT Article 31(3)(c), supplemented by TRIPS). However, I also think that he overstates the political aspect of his claim since there is little evidence to suggest that tribunals are willing to engage in such broad-scope systematising; nor does he adequately show that the case law cited in support of his contention supports those claims based on holdings rather than mere dicta.

### B. Points of Development Beyond Kim
Where Ferguson most clearly develops upon Kim is through the four-risk taxonomy and the four-question expropriation test. Neither of these appears in Kim's monograph in this form. Both of these are directly responsive to the questions Dr. Benavides has asked me since our first meeting (i.e., what "use" looks like in practice, what "disclosure" looks like in practice, and what tribunals should look for to tell whether an asset is being used as a regulatory tool or a confiscatory one if the asset is purely informational). The second major development of Ferguson's work toward the project is his identification of the recognition/enforceability gap in Part II.B. This creates a solid analytical construct that explains why trade secrets are more exposed to theoretical erosion than other forms of intellectual property rights. The development that Ferguson does not contribute to, and which the project will need to develop itself, is a detailed examination of the procedural and evidence-based mechanisms of establishing that there exists a trade secret without causing its destruction.

## VI. Strengths and Weaknesses
### A. Strengths
In terms of contribution, Ferguson makes the greatest contribution with his four-question test for indirect expropriation. He has developed the rare combination of being both analytical (theoretically) and operational (practically), in the form that he has put together the four questions, and an arbitrator, in my opinion, could pick them up tomorrow without needing to rewrite the relevant treaty. These questions are well-ordered, moving from absoluteness, to legal protection, to exclusive use, to commercial value. Most importantly, the answers to all of these questions correspond to existing jurisprudence on substantial interference, so that when an arbitrator answers the questions using the substantial-interference jurisprudence that already exists under international law, he will not need to create any new jurisprudence.

### B. Weaknesses
Ferguson's weakest argument is his treatment of the "territorial nexus" discussed in Part II. He makes an arguably reasonable claim that capital is "relevantly allocated" to a trade secret when that capital is allocated to the disclosure or use of the trade secret in the host State. However, given how much weight this statement will be required to carry as a connection between the recognition argument and the investment argument, the amount of supporting authority provided by Ferguson is insufficient. As such, while the reference to Aplin et al. provides some support for the proposition, and the UK breach-of-confidence analogy found in footnote 114 is likely providing more rhetorical than legal force, a skeptical tribunal may find no obligation to follow Ferguson and the remainder of the analytical structure would suffer accordingly. The research project should consider this the weakest link in Ferguson's argument and seek additional evidence (possibly from the European Trade Secrets Directive; or from recent Court of Justice of the European Union decisions concerning territorial applicability of trade secret protections).

### C. Open Questions for the Project
Three open questions for the project. First: does the four-question test survive contact with a tribunal that holds an aggressive view of police powers? Ferguson gives A11Y as his example of an indirect-expropriation claim defeated by police powers, and he does not work out what his own test would have done on those facts. Burlington runs the other way, and he cites it for exactly that contrast: at ¶ 529 the tribunal found that Ecuador had not validly exercised police powers, because the investor's conduct was justified under municipal law and the State's response was not necessary. So the counter-authority his test has to survive is A11Y alone. That is a narrower problem than I first stated, and a sharper one. Second: how should the project handle Tethyan Copper, which Ferguson uses as his most recent and most useful tribunal data point, but it is partly inaccessible in its unredacted form? Third, and most consequential for Dr. Benavides specifically: is the recognition–enforceability gap a feature or a bug? Ferguson treats it as a feature (it would preserve State sovereignty over the definitional question — though that reading is mine, not his; Ferguson states the requirement and does not evaluate it); a more critical reading would treat it as the structural reason ISDS protection of trade secrets is illusory in the very States most likely to misuse them. In my opinion, this research project should look into this and take a position.

### Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties art. 31

May 23, 1969, 1155 U.N.T.S. 331, <https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/conventions/1_1_1969.pdf>.

**Descriptive annotation.** The general rule of treaty interpretation. Article 31(1) requires a treaty to be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning of its terms in context and in light of object and purpose. Article 31(3)(c) further requires that any relevant rules of international law applicable in the relations between the parties be taken into account. The provision is universally treated as customary international law and applies whether or not the treaty parties are themselves parties to the VCLT.

**Backsourcing verification.** Verified directly against the UN Treaty Collection. The text confirms Ferguson's reliance on Article 31(3)(c) as the systemic-integration mechanism by which TRIPS Article 39 is imported into BIT "investment" definitions; the language of the provision squarely supports his interpretive move.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Article 31(3)(c) is the load-bearing interpretive instrument for the entire "trade secret as investment" pathway and is therefore the doctrinal anchor for the project's broader claim that the existing treaty corpus already protects trade secret holders without requiring redrafting.

### Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights art. 39

Apr. 15, 1994, Marrakesh Agreement Establishing the World Trade Organization, Annex 1C, 1869 U.N.T.S. 299, <https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04d_e.htm>.

**Descriptive annotation.** Article 39 supplies the international floor for trade secret protection, and does so indirectly: it obliges Members to ensure that persons shall have the *possibility of preventing* the specified conduct, which is a duty discharged through municipal law rather than a right conferred directly on the holder. Article 39(2) conditions that possibility on the information being secret, having commercial value because it is secret, and having been subject to reasonable steps to keep it secret. Article 39(1) frames the protection as part of ensuring effective protection against unfair competition under Article 10bis of the Paris Convention; Article 39(3) addresses pharmaceutical and agricultural test data submitted to regulators.

**Backsourcing verification.** Verified directly against the WTO's authoritative text. The three-element definition Ferguson invokes is a faithful distillation of Article 39(2)(a)–(c).

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** TRIPS Article 39 is the textual hook on which Ferguson's definitional argument hangs and is directly responsive to Dr. Benavides' questions about pharmaceutical test data. What Article 31(3)(c) of the VCLT can import into a BIT's investment definition is therefore a *standard that the host State's own law must meet*, not an internationally held entitlement — a weaker bridge than a first reading suggests. Article 39(3) in particular gives the project a treaty-text basis for engaging the Vanda-style fact pattern even though Ferguson himself does not work it through.

### Salini Costruttori S.p.A. v. Kingdom of Morocco

ICSID Case No. ARB/00/4, Decision on Jurisdiction, ¶¶ 52, 57 (July 23, 2001), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/958>.

**Descriptive annotation.** The decision in which an ICSID tribunal articulated the four-criteria test that has come to define the gateway concept of "investment" under ICSID Article 25: a contribution of money or other assets; a certain duration of performance; an element of risk; and a contribution to the economic development of the host State. The four criteria are typically read cumulatively, although subsequent tribunals have varied in how strictly they apply the development prong.

**Backsourcing verification.** Located on italaw and the four-criteria framework verified against the published award and against the consistent secondary literature treating Salini as the leading test. Ferguson's use of the test as the ICSID gateway is accurate; his application to a trade secret turns on whether the secret can satisfy criteria (1) and (4), which is contestable but defensible.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Salini is the procedural threshold any trade-secret-as-investment claim must clear before substantive treaty discipline engages. For the project, it is the framework against which to test whether each subspecies of trade secret (industrial know-how, pharmaceutical test data, geological data, software) plausibly survives the gateway inquiry.

### Fedax N.V. v. Republic of Venezuela

ICSID Case No. ARB/96/3, Decision of the Tribunal on Objections to Jurisdiction, ¶ 43 (July 11, 1997), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/432>.

**Descriptive annotation.** An early ICSID decision in which the tribunal held that promissory notes acquired by a Dutch company on the secondary market constituted a protected "investment" notwithstanding the absence of a classical cross-border financial transfer. The tribunal articulated a flexible understanding of the transfer requirement keyed to whether resources were ultimately committed to the host State, rather than to the form of the transfer.

**Backsourcing verification.** Located on italaw and the holding verified. Ferguson's citation to ¶ 43 for the proposition that some form of transfer between home and host State is required is accurate, although Fedax is a permissive authority and is more useful for the proposition that the transfer can be intangible than for any narrower territoriality requirement.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Useful because trade secrets, like promissory notes, are intangibles whose territorial presence in the host State is contestable. Fedax supports the analytical move whereby a trade secret licensed to a host-State entity satisfies the transfer requirement even though no physical asset crosses a border.

### Saluka Investments BV v. Czech Republic

UNCITRAL, Partial Award, ¶¶ 471–480, 504 (Mar. 17, 2006), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/961>.

**Descriptive annotation.** An UNCITRAL tribunal seated under the Netherlands–Czech Republic BIT held that the Czech Republic violated the BIT's non-impairment obligation by, among other things, leaking confidential information about the financial distress of IPB — a Czech bank in which the Dutch claimant held shares — in a way that frustrated the claimant's ability to participate in the resolution of the bank.

**Backsourcing verification.** Located on italaw; the holding on disclosure-as-treaty-violation is widely confirmed in secondary literature, but **the specific text of ¶¶ 471–480 and 504 could not be extracted from the PDF and has not been read.** The holding is therefore recorded as *reported* rather than verified, and the paragraphs are in the library request. Ferguson's use of Saluka as the leading proof-of-concept that disclosure of confidential commercial information can ground a treaty claim is well-supported by the case as reported.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Saluka is the single most important data point for the disclosure half of Ferguson's four-risk taxonomy and one of the few awards in which a tribunal has actually granted relief on facts close to the trade secret context. What it can be treated as depends on what the unread paragraphs say. ### Alps Finance and Trade AG v. Slovak Republic

UNCITRAL, Award, ¶ 231 (Mar. 5, 2011), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/74>.

**Descriptive annotation.** An UNCITRAL tribunal stated that it is common practice in investment treaty arbitration that an "asset" within the meaning of a BIT means a right or claim having an economic value and deriving either from law or from contract. The tribunal ultimately held that a one-off sales contract did not satisfy the asset definition because it lacked the requisite economic and temporal characteristics.

**Backsourcing verification.** Located on italaw; the asset-definition formulation is correctly attributed. The holding cuts both ways for trade secrets: the formulation is permissive (any right deriving from law or contract qualifies) but its application to the underlying facts is restrictive.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Alps Finance gives the project the most usable working definition of "asset" against which to test whether a particular trade secret amounts to one. The contract pathway is significant, though not as an alternative to statutory protection: confidentiality agreements, non-disclosure agreements and restrictive covenants are the means by which a holder satisfies the statutory condition that reasonable measures were taken to preserve secrecy. They are how one qualifies for protection rather than a substitute for it, and they are evidentiary as much as proprietary — they are what the holder produces to prove the condition was met.

### Celgard, LLC v. Shenzhen Senior Technology Material Co. Ltd

[2020] EWHC 2072 (Ch), ¶¶ 69, 84 (Eng.); aff'd [2020] EWCA Civ 1293, <https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWCA/Civ/2020/1293.html>.

**Descriptive annotation.** The English High Court granted an interim injunction protecting Celgard's trade secrets relating to lithium-ion battery separator technology, even though the alleged misappropriation occurred outside the United Kingdom. The court applied English breach-of-confidence law on the strength of the U.K.-located harmful conduct (sales to U.K. customers) rather than the law of the place of the original taking.

**Backsourcing verification.** Located on BAILII at the appellate level and verified through both the appellate judgment and contemporary IP commentary. The proposition Ferguson cites — that English law applied because the harmful sales were in the U.K. despite the underlying taking being elsewhere — is accurately attributed. Note for scope: this is private litigation between two companies, so the fork-in-the-road doctrine, which operates on an investor's election between a domestic forum and treaty arbitration, had no occasion to arise and was not considered.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Celgard is the cleanest recent municipal-law authority on the territorial recognition question and supports a permissive reading of the Bayview territoriality requirement when read into the trade secret context. It is also the sharpest available illustration of a procedural problem the project should develop: damages compensate for an asset that disclosure has already destroyed, so what the holder characteristically needs is relief that stops disclosure before it happens. The earlier version of this sentence said the national court is the only forum that can act in time and with binding effect, and that treaty-side provisional relief is recommendatory rather than directly enforceable. Both are withdrawn as categorical statements. A treaty tribunal is not powerless: Article 47 of the ICSID Convention permits provisional measures (Rule 47 of the 2022 Arbitration Rules; Rule 39 under the earlier rules), and Kim's own footnote 23 records that "the availability of injunctive relief is not excluded," citing exactly those provisions along with Article 26 of the UNCITRAL Rules and Schreuer on non-pecuniary remedies. What is supportable is comparative and contingent: domestic relief can sometimes be obtained earlier and enforced more directly, treaty-side relief can face constitution-of-the-tribunal delay and compliance limits, and which forum is adequate is rules-, forum-, timing- and fact-specific. Irreversibility remains a genuine concern rather than a rule — Kim states it in the same footnote, that once data is public "the data originator cannot control or prohibit the subsequent use of third parties." If seeking interim relief in one forum were later held to prejudice the other, a holder could be forced to choose between preserving the secret and preserving the claim. Ferguson does not address any of this: a full-text search of the article returns no occurrence of "provisional measure," of ICSID Article 47, or of Rule 39.

### Matalia v. Warwickshire County Council

### Mark A. Lemley, *The Surprising Virtues of Treating Trade Secrets as IP Rights*

61 Stan. L. Rev. 311, 329–41 (2008), <https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Lemley-Surprising.pdf>.

**Descriptive annotation.** Lemley argues, against the conventional wisdom that trade secret law is anomalous within intellectual property doctrine, that treating trade secrets as IP rights resolves longstanding doctrinal puzzles and yields clearer standards for unfair competition than tort or contract framings. The pages cited develop the argument that the IP framing produces better doctrinal outcomes than the leading alternatives.

**Backsourcing verification.** Located in full text on the Stanford Law School website and the central thesis verified directly. Ferguson's citation is accurate and represents the standard reference for the IP-status proposition in U.S. legal scholarship.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Ferguson's first link is treaty text, not Lemley: a BIT's investment definition characteristically enumerates "intellectual property rights"; TRIPS Article 1.2 defines "intellectual property" as all categories in sections 1–7 of Part II; Article 39, on undisclosed information, sits in section 7; so on the TRIPS definition trade secrets are a type of IP. Lemley is cited once, at Ferguson's note 50, as a "theoretical justification" behind Bently, and carries less of the chain than I first gave him. **Link one:** trade secrets sit inside the IP family rather than beside it. **Link two:** BIT investment definitions characteristically enumerate "intellectual property rights," so on that categorisation trade secrets fall within the enumerated category without needing to be named. **Link three:** VCLT Article 31(3)(c) then supplies the category's content from TRIPS Article 39. Two qualifications the project should carry: Lemley is United States domestic IP scholarship, persuasive on categorisation but not authoritative on treaty interpretation, and a tribunal is not bound to adopt a U.S. academic taxonomy when construing a BIT; and what Article 39 supplies is a standard municipal law must meet rather than an internationally held right. The chain holds; each link is weaker than it first appears.

**Descriptive annotation.** Bonnitcha and Aisbett argue that the conventional balancing approach used by ISDS tribunals to distinguish compensable expropriation from non-compensable regulation is doctrinally incoherent and should be replaced with a use/regulation distinction: government use of an investor's property would be compensable; government regulation that does not amount to use would not.
