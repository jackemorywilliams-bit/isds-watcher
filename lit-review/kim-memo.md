## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo will analyze Kim's article based on the original framing question that you handed me at the beginning of this review, namely: do the prospects for investor-state arbitration (ISDS) for a pharmaceutical company's clinical trial test data truly reflect the negative prospects for the investor that Kim indicates they have, and if so why? Kim is the sole source referenced in the reading materials where she has addressed the pharmaceutical disclosure fact pattern on its own terms versus using the pharmaceutical disclosure as just another example in a broad trade secret legal framework. Additionally, while Ferguson is positive that the indirect expropriation doctrine, as interpreted through TRIPS and VCLT, could provide sufficient protections for trade secrets under ISDS, Kim believes otherwise, and that skepticism is directly attributed to the specific regulatory environment of the pharmaceutical industry that the research project is most concerned about. Therefore, I treated Kim as the primary counterpoint to Ferguson, and reviewed her article with focus on what aspects of the clinical-trial data fact pattern she believed would make ISDS protection unlikely.

## II. Source Overview
### A. Argument and Framework
Kim argues that even though pharmaceutical companies could (in theory) make an investment dispute out of their complaints about domestic government regulatory requirements that demand disclosure of clinical trial data, there is little real prospect of success with investor-state arbitration regarding such demands. This is due to the inherent characteristics of clinical trial data as a product of research, and the public welfare nature of the regulatory frameworks which provide for disclosure. In two steps, similar to those taken by Ferguson, Kim arrives at the contrary position. In the first step, Kim addresses the threshold issue of whether clinical trial data constitutes an "investment" within the terms of the various investment treaties and the ICSID Convention, using the Apotex v. United States award and the Philip Morris v. Uruguay case as reference points while noting that neither provides clear-cut conclusions. In the second step, Kim identifies the two most probable substantive standards (indirect expropriation and fair and equitable treatment) and concludes that both are unlikely to support investor claims against regulatory disclosure of clinical trial data for public health or policy innovation reasons.

### B. Case Selection and Scope
Kim's case-base is much smaller than Ferguson's and deals with only a few pharmaceutical-related and pharmaceutical adjacent issues. There are two awards (Apotex v. United States & Philip Morris v. Uruguay) which form Kim's load-bearing ISDS cases; the use of Apotex v. United States is intended by Kim to identify where protection stops for an asset of this kind: an application for marketing approval is the activity of an exporter rather than an investor, and the confidential data inside it does not make it property under NAFTA Article 1139(g). The "outer limits" language in her Part II is not Apotex — it is quoted from Philip Morris v. Uruguay on Article 25(1) of the ICSID Convention, and I had attached it to the wrong case. The second award (Philip Morris v. Uruguay) demonstrates how tribunals will interpret the contribution-to-development criterion where an investor's activity in a host country results in a net-negative effect upon the host economy and where the host State resists jurisdiction. Additionally, Kim refers to Eli Lilly v. Canada, then pending, as providing additional insight into investment claims based on patentability criteria rather than confidentiality or disclosure criteria; the Final Award did not issue until 16 March 2017, so what she had was the claim as pleaded and not the tribunal's treatment of it. Kim also draws on the European Medicines Agency clinical data disclosure policy, and a series of cases decided by the European General Court and Court of Justice as to whether pharmaceutical companies could prevent the EMA from releasing clinical dossiers to the public under EU transparency regulations, namely the InterMune, AbbVie, and PTC Therapeutics litigation. Because Kim wrote in 2016, her work did not cover Vanda Pharmaceuticals v. FDA, which has evolved into the most doctrinally interesting U.S. test case dealing with similar factual circumstances; nor did she take account of the CJEU's final pronouncement on the EMA transparency cases, meaning that some of the open questions she identifies have been addressed since her writing.

## IV. Substantive Analysis
### A. Clinical Data as the Subject Matter of Protection
Kim begins by arguing that clinical data is structurally unique when compared to other forms of intellectual property, and she believes that this structural uniqueness matters for the very first step of the ISDS process — determining whether the clinical data constitutes an "investment" (and therefore is subject to ISDS). Unlike patents and trademarks, which are exclusively owned and therefore easy to apply to the definitions found in BITs, clinical data is only afforded protection against "unfair commercial use" pursuant to Article 39 of the TRIPS Agreement, provided the clinical data meets the standards found in Article 39. Therefore, according to Kim, a tribunal involved in an investor-state dispute regarding clinical data must expend significantly more effort than in a patent dispute to determine whether a protectable asset even exists; furthermore, Kim argues that tribunals in practice have consistently failed to make the necessary efforts. She uses the Apotex award as her clearest evidence of this failure. In Apotex, the UNCITRAL tribunal held that a Canadian generic manufacturer's activities in the U.S. market did not constitute an investment within the meaning of NAFTA Article 1139; accordingly, the tribunal did not need to address the substantive issue of expropriation in order to dispose of the case. My read of this move is that it is analytically sound, but that it depends on a narrower definition of "investment" than Ferguson assumes, and therefore the research project will be required to assess whether Apotex is indicative of tribunal practice generally or simply a result of the specific way Apotex structured its claim. Kim also examines the application of the contribution-to-development criterion from the Salini line of decisions (as a second and connected move), and it runs in the opposite direction to the one I first recorded here. Her anchor is the Philip Morris v. Uruguay Decision on Jurisdiction of 2 July 2013, in which Uruguay argued that the claimants' net contributions to its economy had been overwhelmingly negative and that the contribution-to-development criterion therefore defeated jurisdiction. That argument was dismissed: the tribunal held that the four elements of the Salini list do not constitute jurisdictional requirements, and upheld jurisdiction. What Kim takes from it is that the developmental aspect "has not been perceived by tribunals as a mandatory legal criterion for the purpose of investment definition and protection," and that "[e]ven if it were so, such requirement would not be problematic for IP-based assets" — after which she argues that clinical dossiers can be seen as contributing to the technical capacity of local subsidiaries. The criterion is not a discretionary backstop in her account; it is an objection she treats as having failed and as unlikely to succeed against an IP-based asset. My earlier reading inverted her, and I withdraw it along with the characterization of this as the article's most politically pragmatic section — the tribunal she cites went on to decide the merits.

### B. The Assessment Under the Expropriation Standard
Kim's approach to the application of the indirect expropriation standard is both the most clear-cut departure from Ferguson and the most direct response to Dr. Benavides' framing question. Specifically, she states that when an investor challenges a regulatory disclosure measure based upon the expropriation standard, it will nearly always be difficult for them to meet their evidentiary burden — the investor must show that the regulatory body has caused actual or possible monetary harm from non-commercial utilization of the disclosed data. This can only be done via a counterfactual damages calculation that clinical data does not easily lend itself to. The investor's theory of loss therefore has to rest on speculative downstream impacts on its market position rather than on any direct commercial exploitation of the data by the recipient of the disclosure. As Kim points out, the purpose behind making clinical trial dossiers publicly available via third parties (for example the EMA disclosure policy) is to allow review of those trials for public health purposes or to enable follow-on innovation, and by definition those uses are not commercial. Therefore, according to Kim, a skeptical tribunal would likely treat the disclosure as an ordinary regulatory action falling within the police-powers carve-out, and would consequently treat the investor's loss as a non-compensable regulatory consequence rather than a substantial deprivation of the investment. In my opinion, this is the single strongest piece of analysis in her article and also the best answer to the research question about why ISDS is unfavorable for pharmaceutical investors in this particular fact pattern, in the sense that the structural obstacle is not the doctrine itself but the evidentiary architecture that the doctrine requires an investor to satisfy.

### C. The Assessment Under the FET Standard and "Legitimate Expectations"
Kim's treatment of the fair and equitable treatment (FET) standard was where I anticipated some degree of softness with regard to doctrine. Instead, her analysis provides a tighter articulation than I expected. According to Kim, the most common form of a claim based upon a breach of the FET standard in the context of pharmaceutical disclosure would be the claimant's "legitimate expectations" that its clinical data would remain confidential. However, she states that since there are no express provisions contained within treaties, nor any regulatory assurances or customary administrative practices in place that establish an obligation to maintain confidentiality, the legitimate-expectations doctrine is too vague to support a viable claim, and tribunals have generally refused to imply obligations of confidentiality solely by virtue of general regulatory norms. The TRIPS Article 39(3) regime is applicable to this issue inasmuch as it represents the closest approximation of an enforceable obligation on States at the international level to protect pharmaceutical test data. Kim argues, however, that TRIPS Article 39(3) is constructed as an obligation to prevent unfair commercial use of data rather than as a general non-disclosure obligation. Furthermore, according to Kim, the WTO panel and legislative history support the reading that TRIPS Article 39(3) does not include regulation-based disclosures for public health reasons. In my view, this is the most carefully articulated doctrine-related portion of the article; additionally, it is the area where Kim's work differs the most from Ferguson in terms of doctrine — whereas Ferguson views TRIPS Article 39(3) as part of a bridge doctrine allowing tribunals to provide protection for trade secrets through the expropriation standard, Kim views the same provision as an obstacle to a similar FET claim.

There are two areas in the paper where I believe Kim undercuts her own position relative to how she presents it, and both warrant the attention of the research project rather than being inherited uncritically. First — and this is a correction to what I wrote here, not a criticism of Kim — I recorded a pattern of omitted case outcomes that a full reading does not bear out. While I do not intend to criticize Kim for writing her paper in 2016, the research project will need to incorporate a procedure to confirm whether the post-2016 case law has affected any of the doctrinal pieces that Kim treats as settled.

## V. Relationship to Ferguson
### A. Points of Disagreement with Ferguson
The debate between Kim and Ferguson is real, and it is the biggest conflict the research project has surfaced so far during this phase of the literature review. At the doctrinal level, Kim and Ferguson have examined almost identical treaty architecture — BITs, the ICSID Convention, TRIPS Article 39, and the VCLT interpretive tools — and have come to opposing conclusions. Ferguson believes that with careful application, the existing architecture provides sufficient protections for pharmaceutical trade secrets under the indirect expropriation provisions, and that the failure of tribunals to provide such protections up until now is based on an interpretive inertia that may be overcome through better arbitral reasoning. Kim, on the other hand, reads the architecture as favoring the pharmaceutical investor in no way whatsoever; she argues that there is a systematic disadvantage to investors baked into the interplay between the investor-protection backstops contained within the treaties, which all tend to move in the same direction. She also argues that while tribunals have failed to provide adequate protections for pharmaceutical investors, this is not simply a case of tribunals failing to apply the existing treaty language properly, but rather that the existing treaty structure was never designed to produce those outcomes. My preliminary read is that neither Kim nor Ferguson are actually debating what the treaties state regarding pharmaceutical trade secret protections; instead, they are debating how much leeway should be attributed to tribunals when interpreting treaty language, and whether arbitrators are able or willing to perform the kind of integrative work across multiple treaties that Kim believes they have repeatedly refused to do. The research project will need to decide where it stands on this question and not leave it unresolved. However, based on a fair examination of the cases since 2016, the honest position is closer to Kim than to Ferguson on the politics, even if Ferguson is closer to the right answer on the pure doctrinal pathway. Ferguson's doctrinal advantage is that he correctly identifies the textual tools available within the VCLT and TRIPS that would, if applied as written, permit tribunals to integrate trade secret protections into the expropriation framework; the architecture does support the reading he gives it. Where Ferguson falls short is in assuming that tribunals will actually perform that integrative work when the political cost of doing so in a pharmaceutical case is high, and Kim's account of the evidentiary burden explains why the integrative work would not rescue the claim even if a tribunal performed it, since the investor would still have to prove a counterfactual the asset does not support.

### B. Points of Convergence and Development
Where Kim and Ferguson actually agree, and where the research project can treat the convergence as load-bearing, is on the prior point that clinical data is fundamentally distinct from other forms of intellectual property in ways that impact the jurisdictional threshold at the ISDS level. Both scholars recognize the recognition-and-enforceability issue, and Kim states it as directly as Ferguson does — her own Part II.B holds that "[t]he legal characterization of a foreign investment—tangibles as well as intangibles—is contingent upon securing property rights as recognized under the domestic law of the host state," and that "[t]o claim investment protection of data in a host state, the law of that state should recognize a right in rem in clinical data," both cited to Douglas as Ferguson's is. I earlier called her route indirect; it is not, and the Apotex and Philip Morris analysis illustrates a proposition she states expressly, and both scholars recognize that the TRIPS Article 39(3) regime is the textual centerpiece of any legitimate investor-protection based theory of pharmaceutical data protection. The contribution Kim makes to Ferguson, which I take to be her most valuable addition, is her account of the evidentiary burden the expropriation standard places on a clinical-data claimant. Ferguson recognizes the police-powers and public-welfare limitations on his framework but does not analyze the manner in which they would apply in an actual pharmaceutical dispute. Kim does apply the police-powers carve-out — she predicts a sceptical tribunal would treat a disclosure measure as ordinary regulatory action falling within it. What she declines is the balancing analysis: she names the proportionality question at Part III.B.2 and states that the article does not intend to analyse how proportionality should be implied in balancing investor rights against public interests. What she supplies instead, in considerable detail, is the reason the claim does not survive to be decided on any balance at all: the counterfactual valuation the standard demands is one clinical trial data cannot support. The balancing limb is therefore open in both accounts, and the project inherits it from both rather than resolving it by choosing between them. The area in which Kim has made no such contribution, and one that the research project will still have to seek outside of Kim's work, is a comparable level of detail concerning the procedural and evidentiary mechanisms involved in successfully advancing an ISDS action regarding trade secrets without exposing the secret by doing so; this is the same deficiency I previously identified in the Ferguson memo.

## VI. Strengths and Weaknesses
### A. Strengths
Kim's best contribution is her argument on the evidentiary burden the expropriation standard places on a pharmaceutical claimant. It is the part of the article that most directly answers the framing question Dr. Benavides organized my reading around, and it is articulated with technical care rather than rhetorical flair: it is tightly reasoned and substantiated through reference to the specific structural features of clinical trial data rather than through general claims about deference to regulatory agencies, which makes it a much more analytically useful tool for the research project than the more abstract police-powers literature the research project has otherwise relied on. Her treatment of the disclosure requirements imposed upon pharmaceutical companies by the EMA is what gives the argument its purchase, because it locates the reason the disclosed data has no commercial user in the structure of the disclosure regime itself rather than in a general presumption of regulatory deference, and that gives another researcher a starting point for a new fact pattern without having to rebuild the analytic framework. That kind of practical applicability is rare in the academic literature, and it is the primary reason the article remains viable nearly a decade after its original publication. What the article does not supply is the proportionality analysis I originally credited to it: Kim names the balancing question in Part III.B.2 and states that the article does not intend to analyse it, so the framework set out in Section IV.D above is the project's and not hers.

### B. Weaknesses
Kim's most glaring weak point, in my view, is her selective treatment of case outcomes that I identified in Section IV.E above. In several places throughout the article Kim cites a case to establish the proposition that tribunals have been reluctant to protect clinical data; however, in those same instances, she fails to advise the reader whether the decision was based upon the rationale cited by Kim as evidence of such reluctance, or whether the court determined the issue on a narrower ground that would be inconsistent with her argument. In other words, while she is not misrepresenting the courts' decisions, she is creating rhetorical momentum that moves beyond what the underlying record supports, and a diligent reader doing backsourcing can identify areas of the article where the evidence has left some of these questions unanswered. A further weakness is one of vintage rather than of reasoning: the article is from 2016, and the specific EMA transparency line Kim relies upon has since been adjudicated at the Court of Justice level in a manner that both supports and complicates her position. The research project should therefore consider Kim's 2016 analysis as a good starting point and not a final conclusion regarding the European side of the story.

### C. Open Questions for the Project
Three open questions for the project arise from this memo. First, can Kim's police-powers answer be maintained within the post-2016 judicial landscape governing the EMA transparency regime, and specifically the Court of Justice decisions in the PTC Therapeutics and MSD Animal Health litigation; both of these cases have been decided since the article was written, and therefore the project will have to backsource the findings directly rather than rely on the summary provided by Kim. Second, does Kim's evidentiary-burden argument under the expropriation standard continue to hold up against the fact pattern presented in Vanda Pharmaceuticals v. FDA, which represents the closest post-2016 U.S. analog to Kim's prediction about the type of fact pattern that would fail under the evidentiary-burden approach, but with an added jurisdictional dimension she did not consider. Third, and most importantly for Dr. Benavides, how should the project resolve the differing interpretations offered by Kim and Ferguson as to the same treaty architecture, and in particular whether the research project should take Kim's politically practical interpretation, Ferguson's doctrinal optimism, or a third option in which Ferguson's pathway is taken as a legitimate interpretive path for purposes of legal reasoning while the constraint that controls in practice is the evidentiary one Kim identifies — the counterfactual the claimant cannot prove — which bites before any balancing question is reached. There is a fourth possibility I should have flagged when I first drafted this, which is that neither author reaches the balancing question at all, in which case the proportionality analysis is not a choice between them but a gap in both and the project has to supply it.

### Apotex Holdings Inc. & Apotex Inc. v. United States

ICSID Case No. ARB(AF)/12/1, Award (Aug. 25, 2014), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/96>.

**Descriptive annotation.** A NAFTA Chapter Eleven award in which a Canadian generic-pharmaceutical manufacturer challenged U.S. FDA import-alert and patent-litigation conduct as breaches of NAFTA Articles 1102, 1103 and 1105. The tribunal dismissed the claim on jurisdictional grounds, holding that Apotex's submission of Abbreviated New Drug Applications and its associated U.S. patent litigation did not, on the facts, amount to an "investment" within the meaning of NAFTA Article 1139, and therefore that the dispute did not fall within Chapter Eleven at all. The tribunal did not reach the substantive expropriation or fair-and-equitable-treatment standards.

**Backsourcing verification.** Award independently retrievable from italaw, and Kim's pin cites for the investment-definition discussion match the corresponding paragraphs. The doctrinal proposition for which Kim cites the case — that the tribunal used the investment threshold as a jurisdictional off-ramp without reaching the substantive expropriation question — is supported by the cited paragraphs, but only as to the specific NAFTA 1139 question. The broader proposition Kim attaches, that this reflects a general tribunal practice of avoiding clinical-data expropriation merits questions, is interpretive rather than directly supported by the Award text.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Apotex is the load-bearing case in Kim's investment-threshold argument and therefore the case the project must engage to either side with or push back against her politically realistic reading. The Award's reasoning is more closely tied to the specific way Apotex framed its claim — as a defensive challenge to a U.S. court ruling rather than as a regulatory-disclosure dispute — than her characterization suggests, and the project should be careful not to inherit Apotex as a general proposition about clinical-data investments.

### Philip Morris Brands SARL, Philip Morris Products S.A. & Abal Hermanos S.A. v. Oriental Republic of Uruguay

ICSID Case No. ARB/10/7, Award, ¶¶ 200–210, 287–307 (July 8, 2016), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/460>.

**Descriptive annotation.** An ICSID award in which two Philip Morris affiliates challenged Uruguay's tobacco-control measures — the single-presentation requirement and the 80% graphic-warning requirement — as violations of the Switzerland–Uruguay BIT. The tribunal upheld jurisdiction, including on whether the Salini criteria are jurisdictional prerequisites under the BIT (holding they are not), and then proceeded to the merits, rejecting both the indirect expropriation and fair-and-equitable-treatment claims on the basis that Uruguay's measures were a bona fide exercise of police powers in furtherance of public health.

**Backsourcing verification.** Award independently retrievable on italaw. The Salini-criteria pin cite Kim relies on is at ¶ 206, originally flagged for library assistance and since recovered from the publicly available Award PDF. The pin cite supports the narrow proposition Kim takes from it — that the tribunal expressly declines to treat the Salini criteria as jurisdictional requirements — but the broader proposition she builds on top of it, that this declination amounts to a discretionary backstop tribunals can use to dismiss politically sensitive claims without reaching the merits, is not supported by the cited paragraphs: the tribunal in Philip Morris itself proceeded to the merits and decided on substantive police-powers grounds.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Philip Morris v. Uruguay is a much weaker authority for the jurisdictional argument Kim deploys it for in Section IV.A than for the substantive police-powers reasoning the Award actually turns on, which is the closest tribunal-level analog to the EMA-style fact pattern and is where the doctrinal weight of the Award sits. Kim does not deploy it that way, because she does not develop the balancing analysis at all, so the repositioning is the project's to make rather than a correction of hers. On the superlative: I originally called this the strongest single authority for her proportionality argument, and I withdraw that on two grounds — she has no proportionality argument for it to support, and I had not screened the field the ranking implies.

### Eli Lilly and Company v. Government of Canada

ICSID Case No. UNCT/14/2, Final Award (Mar. 16, 2017), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/1625>.

**Descriptive annotation.** A UNCITRAL/NAFTA award in which Eli Lilly challenged the Canadian Federal Court's invalidation of two pharmaceutical patents under the Canadian promise doctrine of patent utility. The tribunal rejected the claim on the merits, holding both that the Canadian doctrine did not amount to a dramatic and arbitrary change in the law and that it would in any event be reluctant to second-guess the substantive reasoning of domestic courts on patentability.

**Backsourcing verification.** Final Award independently retrievable on italaw and read in full for this entry. Kim, writing in 2016, cited the case pre-award — the Final Award issued 16 March 2017 — so her references are to the claim as pleaded, and this entry does not verify any pin cite of hers against the Award text; the earlier version of this entry stated that it did, and that was wrong. What the Award does support, on the project's own reading rather than on hers, is the narrow patentability proposition that tribunals defer to domestic courts on substantive IP standards even where the result disfavours the foreign investor. The extension from patentability to clinical-data confidentiality is not supported by the Award text: the tribunal did not address confidentiality or disclosure and would have had no occasion to. Screening the full Award confirms the point mechanically — "proportional" and "police power" each occur zero times, so the Award decides nothing on a balancing or carve-out rationale; it turns on failure of proof (¶¶ 376, 435, 437, 469) and never reaches damages (¶ 472).

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Useful as the most recent ISDS data point on how tribunals treat domestic IP doctrine, and directly relevant to any prediction about a tribunal asked to override a regulator's disclosure decision. The case cuts in Kim's favour more than Ferguson's: tribunal deference to domestic IP determinations is exactly the disposition she predicts and exactly the one he hopes interpretive work can overcome. It should be treated as evidence on the empirical side of the disagreement rather than as authority on the doctrinal side.

### Salini Costruttori S.p.A. & Italstrade S.p.A. v. Kingdom of Morocco

ICSID Case No. ARB/00/4, Decision on Jurisdiction, ¶ 52 (July 23, 2001), <https://www.italaw.com/cases/958>.

**Descriptive annotation.** An ICSID jurisdictional decision arising from a construction-contract dispute over a highway project, best known for its formulation of the four-part Salini test for whether a transaction qualifies as an "investment" under ICSID Article 25(1): a contribution of money or other assets, a certain duration, an element of risk, and a contribution to the host State's economic development.

**Backsourcing verification.** Decision retrievable on italaw; Kim's pin cite to ¶ 52 is accurate and the cited language supports the proposition that the four criteria exist. The application Kim makes of those criteria to clinical data is interpretive and not directly supported by the Salini text: Salini is a construction-contract dispute in which the contribution-to-development question had a relatively concrete answer, which does not obviously translate to an asset whose contribution to host-state development is contested at the level of definition.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Salini is the gateway test any clinical-data claimant must clear before substantive treaty standards become available, and therefore the terrain on which most of Kim's predicted tribunal practice would play out. The analytical work she attributes to the criteria in the pharmaceutical setting is the same interpretive bridging Ferguson believes tribunals could do in the investor's favour and she believes they will not — which makes Salini, in a sense, the place where the disagreement has to be litigated.

### ICSID Convention art. 25(1)

Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes Between States and Nationals of Other States, Mar. 18, 1965, 17 U.S.T. 1270, 575 U.N.T.S. 159, <https://icsid.worldbank.org/sites/default/files/ICSID%20Convention%20English.pdf>.

**Descriptive annotation.** Article 25(1) sets the basic jurisdictional reach of ICSID arbitration, extending it to any legal dispute arising directly out of an investment between a Contracting State and a national of another Contracting State where the parties have consented in writing. The Convention does not define "investment," and what counts as one for Article 25(1) purposes has been the subject of extensive subsequent tribunal and academic interpretation, including the Salini line.

**Backsourcing verification.** Convention text independently retrievable from the ICSID website; Kim's citation is accurate. The cited language supports the bare jurisdictional proposition that ICSID jurisdiction depends on the existence of an investment, but does not on its own support the substantive proposition she builds on top of it: Article 25(1) is famously underdetermined, and the analytical work she attributes to the Article is in fact being done by the tribunals in Apotex, Philip Morris and the Salini line rather than by the Convention itself.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Article 25(1) is the textual hook on which the investment-threshold debate hangs, relevant not because it does analytical work of its own but because it is the empty vessel into which both Kim and Ferguson pour their respective tribunal-practice arguments. The project should treat it as the constitutional frame within which the Apotex/Philip Morris/Salini debate takes place, not as load-bearing in its own right.

### Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights art. 39(3)

Apr. 15, 1994, Marrakesh Agreement Establishing the World Trade Organization, Annex 1C, 1869 U.N.T.S. 299, <https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips.pdf>.

**Descriptive annotation.** Article 39(3) requires WTO Members, when conditioning marketing approval of pharmaceutical or agricultural chemical products on the submission of undisclosed test data whose origination involves considerable effort, to protect such data against unfair commercial use, and to protect it against disclosure except where necessary to protect the public or unless steps are taken to ensure protection against unfair commercial use. It is the only express international-law obligation directed specifically at pharmaceutical test data.

**Backsourcing verification.** TRIPS text independently retrievable from the WTO; Kim's quotation is verbatim and the citation accurate. The cited language supports the narrow proposition that the Article is structured around unfair commercial use rather than confidentiality as such, which is the proposition Kim builds her FET analysis on. The travaux préparatoires are more contested than Kim acknowledges, and there is a meaningful body of secondary literature reading Article 39(3) more broadly, which the project should note when treating her reading as the only available one.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Article 39(3) is the doctrinal hinge of Kim's FET analysis and the textual point at which she and Ferguson most directly disagree: he reads it as part of a bridge by which TRIPS protections can be ported into the BIT framework, she reads it as a textual obstacle to the analogous legitimate-expectations argument. Note the structural feature that bears on both readings — the obligation runs to Members to make protection available through municipal law, so what can be imported is a standard the host State's law must meet rather than an internationally held right.

### European Medicines Agency, Policy 0070 on Publication of Clinical Data for Medicinal Products for Human Use

EMA/144064/2019 (originally adopted Oct. 2, 2014, revised effective 2019), <https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/other/european-medicines-agency-policy-publication-clinical-data-medicinal-products-human-use_en.pdf>.

**Descriptive annotation.** The EMA's clinical-data publication policy, providing for proactive publication of clinical study reports and other clinical-trial documents submitted by marketing-authorization applicants. The policy rests on a tiered framework distinguishing commercially confidential information and protected personal data from information the EMA considers releasable in the public interest, and it is the regulatory regime around which Kim builds her central fact pattern.

**Backsourcing verification.** Policy independently retrievable from the EMA website. Kim's descriptions are accurate as of the 2014 version she was working from. The 2019 procedural revisions and the EMA's suspension of new clinical-data publication during its IT-system migration are not addressed by her, for the obvious reason that they post-date the article, but they materially affect how the policy operates in practice and the project should not treat her account as the operative state of the regime today.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Policy 0070 is the closest real-world analog to the abstract compelled-disclosure hypothetical Kim works through, and the regime out of which any clinical-data-disclosure ISDS claim against an EU member state would most plausibly arise. The project cannot evaluate her evidentiary-burden argument without first understanding how the policy operates today, and this entry should be paired with the PTC Therapeutics line below as a single composite source on the European fact pattern.

### InterMune UK Ltd. v. European Medicines Agency

Case T-73/13 R, Order of the President of the General Court (Apr. 25, 2013), <https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=T-73/13>.

**Descriptive annotation.** An interim-measures order in which the President of the EU General Court declined to suspend the EMA's intended release of clinical-trial documents relating to InterMune's pirfenidone authorization in response to a third-party access-to-documents request. The order addressed only the prima facie case and balance-of-interests questions on which interim relief turns, and did not reach the substantive question whether the requested documents fell within the commercial-confidentiality exception.

**Backsourcing verification.** Order independently retrievable on Curia. Kim's citation is accurate as a citation, but the cited paragraphs support only the narrow procedural proposition that interim relief was denied — not the broader substantive proposition that the General Court has held against pharmaceutical claimants on the commercial-confidentiality merits, since that question was never reached. This is one of the entries flagged during first reading, and backsourcing confirms the concern.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** A useful entry in the running case list as an early indicator of the General Court's posture, but the project should not rely on Kim's characterization as authority for any substantive holding, and should treat InterMune as part of the procedural backdrop against which the later PTC Therapeutics line was decided.

### AbbVie Inc. & AbbVie Ltd. v. European Medicines Agency

Case T-44/13, Order of the President of the General Court (Apr. 25, 2013), <https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=T-44/13>; case removed from the General Court register following confidential settlement, Order of Sept. 17, 2014.

**Descriptive annotation.** An interim-measures order in which AbbVie sought to prevent the EMA from disclosing clinical-trial data relating to its arthritis biologic in response to third-party access-to-documents requests. As with InterMune, the President addressed only the prima facie case and balance-of-interests questions. The substantive merits proceeding was never decided: AbbVie and the EMA reached a confidential settlement and the case was removed from the register, so there is no merits-level judgment on the AbbVie commercial-confidentiality question.

**Backsourcing verification.** The interim-measures order and the subsequent register-removal order are both independently retrievable on Curia. Kim's citation is accurate as to the interim order, but the cited material does not support the substantive proposition she uses it for: there is no substantive holding to support, and she does not tell the reader the case settled before reaching the merits. This is the case where the omission-of-outcome problem flagged in Section IV.E is most acute.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Included not as substantive authority but as a flag: AbbVie is one of three cases Kim presents as if they form a coherent body of unfavourable General Court precedent, and the actual procedural posture undermines that framing. The project should engage her characterization critically and should not rely on her summary without also flagging that the case ended in settlement rather than judgment.

### PTC Therapeutics International Ltd. v. European Medicines Agency

Case C-175/18 P, Judgment of the Court (Fourth Chamber) (Jan. 22, 2020), ECLI:EU:C:2020:23, <https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-175/18>; affirming Case T-718/15, Judgment of the General Court (Feb. 5, 2018), ECLI:EU:T:2018:66.

**Descriptive annotation.** A Court of Justice judgment holding that there is no general presumption of confidentiality covering clinical study reports submitted to the EMA, and that an applicant seeking to prevent disclosure under the commercial-confidentiality exception must demonstrate, on a document-by-document basis, that disclosure would specifically and actually undermine its commercial interests. This is the doctrinal pronouncement Kim anticipated but did not have when writing in 2016.

**Backsourcing verification.** Both the General Court and Court of Justice judgments are independently retrievable on Curia. Kim could not have cited the post-2018 procedural history. The 2020 holding runs in the direction of her evidentiary prediction and forecloses the strongest version of the legitimate-expectations argument she analyzes: the document-by-document specific-and-actual-harm requirement is the evidentiary architecture she predicts will be fatal to investor claims. It says nothing about proportionality as she frames it, because she does not frame it — the reading that the judgment confirmed a proportionality intuition of hers was mine and is withdrawn. The post-2016 holdings materially update the landscape she describes and should be treated as controlling on the European side.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** The single most important post-2016 development for both Kim's analysis and the broader research question: the closest the European appellate courts have come to a definitive pronouncement on the relationship between EMA clinical-data disclosure and the commercial-confidentiality interests of pharmaceutical originators, pointing in the direction Kim predicted rather than the direction Ferguson hoped for. The project should backsource the judgment directly rather than rely on her pre-2018 prediction or any commentary paraphrasing it.

### Vanda Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Food & Drug Administration

No. 19-cv-301 (JDB), Mem. Op. (D.D.C. May 6, 2021), ECF No. 65 [parallel reporter citation unverified].

> **Entry corrected 2026-08-03.** The prior version of this entry cited 436 F. Supp. 3d 256 (D.D.C. 2020) and described it as concerning the FDA's public release of clinical-trial information and the rejection of Trade Secrets Act challenges. That was wrong. See the verification statement below.

**Descriptive annotation.** On a non-party law firm's motion to intervene and unseal the administrative record, the court applied the common-law right of access and the Hubbard factors, permitting intervention and partially granting unsealing while allowing Vanda to redact or withhold proprietary information to avoid competitive harm. It held that the confidentiality factor weighed strongly in favour of keeping trade secrets and proprietary information under seal and denied unsealing as to the toxicology reports, but required Vanda to identify specific interests document by document, generalised assertions of confidentiality being insufficient. In assessing the property interest at stake the court drew on the D.C. Circuit line recognising that competitors could use information in drug applications to avoid much of the effort otherwise required to bring a competing product to market.

**Backsourcing verification.** Slip opinion read via govinfo and authenticated against the public docket; **the parallel reporter citation is unverified and the case is cited in docket form pending confirmation.** The separate opinion at 436 F. Supp. 3d 256 (D.D.C. 2020), previously cited here, is an Administrative Procedure Act challenge to a partial clinical hold on tradipitant; it contains no occurrence of trade secret, confidential, proprietary or disclosure, and should be cited only for the deference point, not for any disclosure holding.

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** The closest post-2016 U.S. analog to the European fact pattern, and it cuts both ways. It **confirms** Kim in that a court protected clinical data on secrecy grounds and denied unsealing of the toxicology reports. It **complicates** her in that the D.C. Circuit line recognises a cognisable property interest in application contents, so disclosure is not treated as costlessly non-compensable. The transferable observation — an inference from the opinion, not its holding, which contains no reference to investment law or arbitration — concerns where the evidentiary burden falls: Kim locates the claimant's difficulty at valuation, while this court located it one step earlier, at identification. If that pattern carries into the treaty context, the pharmaceutical claimant's first problem is not quantifying loss but particularising the secret, which is the same paradox from the other direction. Note too a disclosure vector neither scholar models: judicial transparency, a third party using court-access doctrine to reach material the regulator was itself barred from releasing.

### Christoph Schreuer et al., *The ICSID Convention: A Commentary*; Zachary Douglas, *The International Law of Investment Claims*

Schreuer 117–134 (2d ed., Cambridge University Press 2009); Douglas 161–204 (Cambridge University Press 2009).

**Descriptive annotation.** Two leading treatises. Schreuer is the canonical commentary on the ICSID Convention, and the cited pages address the Article 25(1) investment question, including the development of the Salini criteria and tribunal-level approaches to applying them. Douglas is the leading monograph on the architecture of investment-claim adjudication, and the cited pages contain the most thorough critique in the secondary literature of the discretionary use of the Salini criteria as a jurisdictional filter, arguing that tribunals should not treat Salini as load-bearing in the way subsequent practice has come to do.

**Backsourcing verification.** Both treatises retrievable through the law library catalog. Kim's citation to Schreuer is accurate and the cited pages support the propositions for which she uses them. **Douglas is not cited by Kim at all and is included here as a recommended addition rather than as a verification of Kim.**

**Relevance to the Benavides project.** Together these do most of the secondary-literature work the project needs on the investment-threshold question, and the contrast between them captures the Kim–Ferguson disagreement at treatise level rather than at the level of individual awards. Douglas's framing strengthens Kim's politically realistic point even as it cuts against her doctrinal one: he argues tribunals *should not* use Salini as a discretionary filter and she argues that they *do*, so the two together describe both the "ought" and the "is" of the investment-threshold question.
