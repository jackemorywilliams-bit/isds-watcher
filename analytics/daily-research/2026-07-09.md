**China-Netherlands BIT (2001) concurrent-proceedings architecture — asymmetric and conditionally withdrawal-permitting:**

The China-Netherlands BIT (2001) has an asymmetric Article 10 concurrent-proceedings architecture:
- **For disputes concerning investments in China (Chinese host state):** A dispute submitted to a Chinese domestic court may subsequently be submitted to international dispute settlement, but only if the investor withdraws the domestic case before a judgment on the subject matter is delivered. This is a **conditional reversal / withdrawal-permitting** clause — more permissive than a pure fork-in-the-road (FITR), but still requires withdrawal before judgment.
- **For disputes concerning investments in the Netherlands (Dutch host state):** An investor may choose to submit a dispute to international dispute settlement **at any time** — no concurrent-proceedings bar applies.

Sources: [(UNCTAD Navigator, China-Netherlands BIT 2001)](https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/international-investment-agreements/treaties/bilateral-investment-treaties/946/china---netherlands-bit-2001-); [(Springer, Netherlands International Law Review)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40802-020-00182-3); [search-engine summary of BIT text; Article 10 primary text not directly read — MEDIUM confidence on asymmetric architecture characterization].

**Ring 3 implication for Wingtech:** Wingtech's investment is in the Netherlands (Nexperia B.V. is a Dutch company). Under the China-Netherlands BIT (2001), the "investment in Netherlands" branch of Article 10 applies — Wingtech may submit to international dispute settlement at any time, without concurrent-proceedings withdrawal requirements. The parallel Chinese (AFSL/Dongguan) and Dutch (Enterprise Chamber, June 2026 suit) domestic proceedings would need to be assessed against a "triple identity test" (same parties, cause, and object) if the Netherlands invokes any FITR defense. The Chinese AFSL claim (against Nexperia entities and three individuals; AFSL-law basis; Chinese court) and the BIT claim (against Netherlands state; BIT/investment treaty basis; ICSID) are different parties, different legal basis, and different forum — the triple identity test would likely not be met. Sources: [(Springer, Netherlands International Law Review — triple identity test)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40802-020-00182-3); [(CELIS Institute, Nexperia BIT analysis)](https://www.celis.institute/celis-institute/balancing_economic_security_investment_protection_nexperia_china-nl_bit/) [403-blocked; framing from prior project search result].

**Contextual note — AFSL as offensive tool mirroring China's defensive toolkit:**

China's use of the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law in this context mirrors, in an inverse and offensive direction, the domestic legal mechanisms that China has previously used as *defensive* tools in ISDS. In *Jason Yu Song v. China* (PCA 2019-39), China obtained a domestic criminal court judgment against the investor during arbitration and then used it as a post-award set-aside ground (rejected by the Swiss FSC). Here, China's AFSL enables a Chinese outbound investor to subject foreign companies and their officers to Chinese domestic jurisdiction. Both mechanisms (criminal courts for defensive post-award attacks; AFSL for offensive claims against foreign entities) represent China's expansion of domestic legal jurisdiction as part of a BIT dispute strategy. This is an analytical observation — no practitioner source draws this connection.

### 2B. Hela Schwarz — BIT Protocol Article Partially Identified

**Prior record:** STATE_OF_THE_ANSWER and the open thread state "Specific BIT article unconfirmed — treaty PDF 403-blocked throughout."

**New finding:** Multiple secondary sources now identify the specific provision governing concurrent proceedings in the China-Germany BIT (2003) as **Article 9 of the Protocol** to the BIT. The case involved "Article 9(c) of the Protocol," which "relates to addressing how concurrent proceedings should be handled between domestic litigation and international arbitration." Sources: search-engine summary of [(Oxford Academic, JIDS, "Parallel Proceedings under Chinese BITs: The Case of Hela Schwarz GmbH v PR China," Vol. 11, No. 2, p. 335 (2020))](https://academic.oup.com/jids/article-abstract/11/2/335/5807886); search-engine summary of [(SCIRP, "No-U-Turn Clauses in Chinese Investment Treaties")](https://www.scirp.org/pdf/blr_2024022913362295.pdf) [both 403-blocked; reference from search-engine snippet]. The "(c)" sub-provision identification is from a single source and should be treated as LOWER confidence than the "Article 9 of Protocol" identification, which appears consistently across multiple search summaries.

**Doctrinal classification from literature:** Academic secondary sources consistently describe Chinese BIT concurrent-proceedings provisions (including the one at issue in Hela Schwarz) as **"no-U-turn clauses"** rather than pure fork-in-the-road (FITR) clauses. A pure FITR makes domestic court submission irrevocable; a no-U-turn clause allows the investor to return to international arbitration only if they have not yet received a domestic court judgment on the merits (i.e., withdrawal before judgment is the cure pathway). This is more permissive than a FITR but still effectively bars concurrent litigation. Source: [(SCIRP, "No-U-Turn Clauses in Chinese Investment Treaties", 2024)](https://www.scirp.org/pdf/blr_2024022913362295.pdf); [(Jusmundi, "An Analysis of Concurrent Proceedings under Chinese BITs")](https://jusmundi.com/en/document/publication/en-an-analysis-of-concurrent-proceedings-under-chinese-bits) [both 403-blocked; characterizations from search-engine summaries; MEDIUM confidence].

**Practical conclusion (unchanged from prior record):** Whether "fork-in-the-road" or "no-U-turn," the Hela Schwarz Ring 3 bar operates the same way for a pharmaceutical investor: domestic court proceedings in China must be withdrawn (or never commenced) before ICSID filing. The specific article is now: **Article 9 of the Protocol to the China-Germany BIT (2003)**, sub-provision (c) [MEDIUM confidence — secondary sources; primary text still unread].
