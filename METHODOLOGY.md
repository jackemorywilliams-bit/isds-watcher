## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo will detail the operational methodology of the ISDS Thematic Watcher. The ISDS Thematic Watcher is an automated monitoring tool I developed for this project, built through Claude AI and Claude Code as the development medium. Each operational decision made during the development of the ISDS Thematic Watcher was grounded in the literature reviewed up until now. In addition to documenting the methodology, this memo addresses an operational issue relevant to one of the project's substantive questions being addressed by the project. That operational question is: How are changes in investor-State dispute settlement practice over time to be identified within a particular area of law, specifically when an intellectual property right is asserted as a covered investment, a governmental act or court decision is challenged as a violation of that right, and a rule of law is applied at the jurisdictional/admissibility level that determines that the case cannot proceed; and how may that be done repeatedly and uniformly? Rather than addressing the substance of these issues — whether trade secrets and clinical trial data qualify as protected investments — this memo focuses on the methodological approach used to identify those areas of law that meet these criteria. As such, the Ferguson and Kim memos should be referenced for arguments on what rights are entitled to protection under international investment agreements. What this memo describes is how the ISDS Thematic Watcher takes that legal framework and turns it into a repeatable process with multiple points of potential auditability — including where necessary, contestation. Following an introduction to the theoretical basis of the ISDS Thematic Watcher (Part II) the structure of sources used by the instrument (Part III) and the sourcing mechanism employed for collecting data for use in candidate selection and processing (Part III), candidate identification/enrichment (Part IV), a two-step classification cascade (Part V), scoring/calibration/validation (Part VI), standards for reporting regarding results presented in the digest (Part VII), reproducibility (Part VIII) and a candid discussion of limitations (Part IX) are discussed.


## II. Theoretical Framework — The Three-Ring Thematic Fingerprint

### A. Why a thematic instrument, and not a keyword search
The purpose of surveillance is not a single term, but rather a relationship between three doctrines and the relationships among those doctrines. Such a relationship can't be distilled down into a single keyword and still retain the substance of what makes up that relationship. A given case could reference the term "patent" one hundred times and yet still have no relevance; or a case using the term "abuse of right" not at all, may fit perfectly within the category. Therefore, the methodological issue here is interpretative and not lexical in nature, and this is the type of question qualitative methodology was designed to address: How do you train a tool to identify a particular category when it has only had three instances to see? In my efforts to treat the development of the fingerprint as an example of theory-informed sampling and most-similar-cases methodology (which Linos & Carlson advocate for as part of qualitative methods scholars should incorporate into legal writing because legal authors generally select their examples in an un-systematic manner and apply solely doctrinal analysis to these selected cases) (Linos & Carlson, Qualitative Methods for Law Review Writing, 84 U. Chi. L. Rev. 213 (2017)), I did not create the initial seed-corpus as representative of all ISDS, but created the seed-corpus based upon structural similarities along the dimensions of interest for the logic of a most-similar-case methodology.

### B. The seed corpus
Three awards were chosen based upon this commonality of form among three very disparate subject areas - each award exhibits the structural characteristics the project aims to identify: In Philip Morris Asia Ltd v. Commonwealth of Australia, PCA Case No. 2012-12, Award on Jurisdiction and Admissibility (Dec. 17, 2015), an Australian law requiring all cigarettes sold there be packaged with no brand or logo identifiers was challenged by Philip Morris under its treaty-based intellectual property protections. The Arbitral Tribunal dismissed the claim on abuse of right, because Philip Morris had restructured into a Hong Kong entity to acquire treaty rights over a reasonably foreseeable dispute. Eli Lilly & Co. v. Government of Canada, ICSID Case No. UNCT/14/2, Final Award (16 Mar. 2017). This challenged the Canadian courts' decision to invalidate Eli Lilly's patent(s) for its product(s) under the "promise utility doctrine," a judicial measure, pursuant to Article 1105 of the NAFTA minimum standards for treatment of foreign investments, framed as a denial of justice; unlike Philip Morris, this claim was reached and dismissed on the merits, the tribunal finding no dramatic change in Canadian patent law and nothing arbitrary or discriminatory in the utility requirement (Eli Lilly & Co. v. Canada, Final Award (Mar. 16, 2017), italaw case 1625, https://www.italaw.com/cases/1625; IISD, Investment Treaty News (June 12, 2017), https://www.iisd.org/itn/2017/06/12/highly-anticipated-nafta-award-rejects-patent-law-related-claim-against-canada-matthew-levine/). In Bridgestone Licensing Services, Inc. & Bridgestone Americas, Inc. v. Republic of Panama, ICSID Case No. ARB/16/34, Award (Aug. 14, 2020), Bridgestone claimed against Panama that a judgment of Panama's Supreme Court was a denial of justice. In addition, Bridgestone raised the holding-versus-operating-entity question of whether the trademark licences were a covered investment — a jurisdictional/standing issue echoing the corporate-structure concerns in Philip Morris Asia Ltd — but the tribunal resolved that threshold question largely in the claimants' favor at the expedited-objections stage and dismissed the denial-of-justice and fair-and-equitable-treatment claims on the merits, finding no denial of justice and no proven loss (Bridgestone v. Panama, Award (Aug. 14, 2020), italaw case 4475, https://www.italaw.com/cases/4475; Decision on Expedited Objections (Dec. 13, 2017), https://jusmundi.com/en/document/decision/en-bridgestone-americas-inc-and-bridgestone-licensing-services-inc-v-republic-of-panama-decision-on-expedited-objections-wednesday-13th-december-2017). The indexing unit for the above is the arbitrators' own language — the operative phrases were taken directly from the awards (as evidenced by the full per-ring extraction shown in PLAN.md). The selection is intentional and is consistent with one of the basic principles of information retrieval; namely, that index terms most likely to distinguish relevant documents are the ones appearing in those documents themselves (Salton & McGill, Introduction to Modern Information Retrieval (1983); Manning et al., Introduction to Information Retrieval (2008)).

The literature-review seeds are two memos rather than awards. Archived in full at `lit-review/`, the Ferguson and Kim pieces are what prompted the research question in the first place, and they are what Ring 1 actually rests on; they do not add a fourth case so much as supply the doctrinal stakes the three awards leave implicit. Ferguson's *Trade Secrets at Risk* takes the optimistic view. Trade secrets, he argues, are the most exposed form of intellectual property, yet the existing regime already protects them as covered investments once it is read correctly — the asset constituted through TRIPS Article 39 and brought in via VCLT Article 31(3)(c), the threat sorted into a four-part taxonomy of disclosure, use, regulation, and their combinations, and the expropriation question reduced to four: is the State's protection absolute, has there been significant interference with the secret's legal protection, with its exclusive commercial use, and a substantial reduction in its commercial value. Underneath all of it sits what he calls the recognition/enforceability gap — international law protects only what the host State's own municipal law already recognizes. Kim's *Protecting Trade Secrets under International Investment Law* is the deliberate answer to that optimism. Reasoning from pharmaceutical clinical-trial data, she finds ISDS protection unlikely on four counts: clinical data sits awkwardly at the threshold "investment" question (Apotex v. United States fixes the outer limit; Philip Morris v. Uruguay shows the Salini contribution-to-development criterion turned into a discretionary jurisdictional backstop); the expropriation standard demands a counterfactual damages showing that non-commercial disclosure makes nearly impossible; the fair-and-equitable-treatment route through "legitimate expectations" is too thin to imply confidentiality; and and TRIPS Article 39(3), properly read, forbids unfair commercial use rather than disclosure as such, which leaves the proportionality and police-powers carve-out free to treat a public-health disclosure as non-compensable. That disagreement — Ferguson's interpretive optimism against Kim's structural skepticism, argued over the very same treaty architecture of BITs, the ICSID Convention, TRIPS Article 39, and the VCLT — is the spine of the research question. And it is the memos, not any single award, that give Ring 1 both its working vocabulary (trade secret, clinical trial data, test data, data exclusivity, unfair commercial use, TRIPS Article 39(3)) and the reason any of it is worth surfacing.

### C. The three rings
The first ring — Intellectual property as a protected investment. This ring is the first of the rings in relation to whether and under what conditions intellectual property will be treated as a "covered investment" — the defining issue that is repeatedly addressed throughout the entire project's readings (Mercurio, Awakening the Sleeping Giant, 15 J. Int'l Econ. L. 871 (2012); Correa & Viñuales, Intellectual Property Rights as Protected Investments: How Open are the Gates?, 19 J. Int'l Econ. L. 91 (2016)). This particular ring is the greatest when there is information-based assets involved; when there is a distinction between a holding company and an operational company involved, such as with Bridgestone, and where it represents the same structural vulnerability identified by the Ferguson and Kim memos regarding trade secrets and clinical trial data. The defining issue has also imported the Salini criteria — contribution, duration, risk, and contribution to the host economy — from which tribunals possess significant latitude (compare Salini Costruttori S.p.A. v. Morocco, ICSID Case No. ARB/00/4). Triggers for ring one include: covered investment; promise utility doctrine; exploitation of the trademark.
Ring two — A regulatory or judicial action as challenged behavior (weighted). Of the three seeds discussed above, two of them were brought against domestic courts rather than governmental actions. Both of those cases claimed violation of due process based on allegations of denial of justice. The standard used to determine if a state is responsible for its judiciary is set forth in Paulsson, Denial of Justice in International Law (2005) — i.e., a state may be held accountable for its judiciary only upon a showing of manifest injustice; mere error does not meet that test. The seed tribunals use virtually identical language in their decisions to describe conduct that was outrageous and egregious, manifestly unjust judgments, and rulings that were so incorrect they would have been unreasonable and incompetent for any court to reach. Due to the fact that the seed corpus emphasizes this ring significantly, the weight assigned to this ring is equally significant. Thus, a claimant alleging a wrongful decision made by a court can achieve the medium level simply on the basis of that allegation alone. This was a deliberate trade-off made at the scoring-design stage rather than an oversight: weighting the judicial-measure ring this heavily does risk under-reading an IP-as-investment case that reaches the threshold by a purely administrative route, but because this ring on its own can only ever reach the medium level and never the high level, the weighting widens recall without allowing any single doctrine to manufacture a top-band hit, and the residual blind spot is carried forward openly in Part IX. There are several acknowledged grounds for using prevalence of claims within a coding corpus to establish weight for a category (Saldana, Coding Manual). Triggers for ring two include: denial of justice; manifestly unjust judgment; minimum standard of treatment.
Ring three — Threshold questions of jurisdiction and admissibility as a potentially dispositive dimension. This ring watches the dimension on which a case can be turned away before its merits are reached: abuse of right, treaty-shopping, foreseeability of the dispute, restructuring for treaty protection, and the contested definition of the "investor" or "investment." Among the seeds this dimension is anchored by Philip Morris v. Australia, the one that was actually disposed of here — held inadmissible as an abuse of right without ever reaching the merits. It was present but not dispositive in Bridgestone, where the covered-investment/standing question was resolved largely in the claimants' favor and the case turned on the merits; and it was not the basis of decision in Eli Lilly, which was dismissed on the merits. The point is that these threshold questions can be dispositive — and were in Philip Morris — not that all three seeds were decided on them. The most recent development controlling the synthesis is Baumgartner, Treaty Shopping in International Investment Law (2016), which relies on an older abuse-of-right principle (Byers, Abuse of Rights: An Old Principle, A New Age, 47 McGill L.J. 389 (2002)), along with standards found in authoritative treatise writers (Dolzer and Schreuer, Principles of International Investment Law (2nd edition 2012); McLachlan et al., International Investment Arbitration: Substantive Principles (2nd edition 2017)). Triggers for ring three include: abuse of right; reasonably foreseeable; critical date; shell subsidiary.

### D. The scoring grammar
The scoring grammar — the signal that the instrument was designed to recognize is the crossing of the bands. This gives us our scoring grammar. HIGH (70+) requires two rings; MEDIUM (40-69) requires either one strong band and a weaker second tie or just a judicial-measure case by itself. LOW (less than 40) is one weak ring or zero. Extractives, sovereign debt, and intra-EU energy default to LOW unless there is an IP or judicial angle. The full machine-readable frame — every phrase and weight, summing to 100 within each ring — is maintained in fingerprint.yaml. Each phrase is matched as a case-insensitive substring of the item's combined title, summary, and body text rather than as a whole word, so a trigger fires regardless of the surrounding phrasing; heavily inflected terms are caught by registering a truncated stem (for example, "expropriat-"), though the matcher stops short of full lemmatization or regular-expression patterns, a simplification recorded in Part IX.


## III. Source Architecture & Research Methods
I discuss the approach I took to select my data sources and to ensure the reliability of those selections. I first explain how I identified appropriate sources by using multiple different types of sources. In particular, I identify four "channels" through which I collected information about investments disputes. Those channels include institutional documents, archives, analysis, and practitioners. The specific open repositories consulted are: the ICSID docket; UNCTAD's Investment Dispute Settlement Navigator and World Investment Report; the italaw primary-document archive; IISD's Investment Treaty News; and IAReporter at headline level. To these the project later added two further channels: the operator's own Google Alerts feeds, polled as RSS to surface news of pending and seed-case developments, and Google Scholar alerts, read by the instrument from a dedicated mailbox to surface new scholarship engaging the seed cases and doctrines. To be precise about depth of access, these channels fall into three tiers: read in FULL — the ICSID docket, UNCTAD, italaw, IISD Investment Treaty News, and the operator's own Google Alerts and Google Scholar feeds, whose linked pages the instrument fetches and reads in full where the publisher allows; read HEADLINE-ONLY — IAReporter, whose body sits behind a paywall, so only the title and lead are scored; and DISABLED — Google News RSS, which is presently blocked by its robots.txt and therefore inactive (honored, not circumvented), together with any individual page denied by robots or login. Because of these tiers, the instrument is best understood as a lead-generation floor — the minimum of relevant developments that surfaced from openly readable text — and not a comprehensive census or monitor of all ISDS activity at the intersection. I describe this approach as being based on the idea of "triangulating," or cross-checking against other sources, which is a fundamental aspect of research design. According to this approach, if you find consistent results across two or more sources, then it is more likely that what you have found reflects real events and patterns in the world and less likely to reflect random errors (artifact), which would tend to produce inconsistent results across multiple sources. I also indicate that I am using the same set of repositories that I use for manual backsourcing, but instead of searching one at a time, I am searching all of them simultaneously. In addition to explaining the process I used to collect the data, I provide additional details regarding the technical aspects of collecting the data. Specifically, I explain that I used software that allows me to collect data from these various online repositories and that uses rules-based programming to extract specific pieces of information from those databases. For example, once the system locates a listing in one of the databases, it will automatically attempt to locate the original text of that dispute resolution proceeding. Once the system has located both the listing and the full-text version of the dispute resolution proceeding, it logs the location of both items along with the date and time when it was accessed. That stable identifier is also what prevents duplication: the seen-state file (state/seen.json) keys every processed item by its source and id, so the same listing is never scored twice. Where a single dispute surfaces across several feeds, those copies are reconciled later at the Research Brief's deduplicated insight ledger rather than at intake; fully merging one dispute across feeds at the scoring stage — by matching party names, dates, and case numbers — remains a known limitation noted in Part IX. Finally, I address issues related to privacy and accessibility. First, I note that I did not collect data from behind paywalls or authentication and that I therefore did not breach any confidentiality agreements. Second, I indicate that I honor web site owners' requests to prohibit certain programs from accessing their sites by reading their robots.txt files, that the instrument waits at least three seconds between requests to one domain, and that it sends an identifying User-Agent. A single, narrowly scoped exception is made for the operator's own subscribed Google Alerts feeds: Google generates these personal feeds for the operator to poll in a feed reader, so retrieving one's own alert subscription, under the same identifying User-Agent and rate limit, is the feed's intended use rather than the third-party site crawling that robots.txt governs.


## IV. Candidate Identification & Enrichment
Simply displaying a list of candidate names without other information, and simply classifying this list "as is," results in noise. Therefore, before being classified, the most likely candidates are first enriched by the tool. This process involves retrieving the original source document, reducing the content of that document to just its readable portion, and scoring every full sentence in that readable text for doctrinal density in order to surface the single most quotable one. That density score weights most heavily how many fingerprint keywords a sentence carries, then how many holding verbs it uses (held, found, dismissed, and the like), then how many evaluative terms it contains (egregious, manifest, denial), while date-heavy and all-capitals lines are discarded so that paragraph numbers and case captions cannot win. The readable body is what the classifier evaluates; the highest-scoring sentence is carried forward as the candidate citable line. In much the same way as a well-placed pin cite helps a researcher quickly locate a relevant reference in a back-sourced bibliography, that line gives the reader at least one sentence worth reading and the digest a verbatim quotation to anchor the entry.


## V. Classification Procedure — A Two-Stage Cascade
Two stage process for classifying — each has its own strengths and weaknesses when considering both recall and precision. Lexical coding (deterministic, transparent) — ranking candidates based on the keywords contained within candidate ring definitions. Each candidate will be ranked using the keyword weights of each keyword present in the vocabulary defined in fingerprint.yaml. This is a classical weighted term matching process (Salton & McGill, 1983). Once candidates have been ranked in order of their scores, the enrichment will be applied to those candidates with the highest rankings. Also, this lexical coding stage can operate completely independently of whether a language model is available or not. Therefore, if no language model is available, the instrument still produces an ordered and inspectable result with scores. Contextual classification (paraphrase tolerant) — evaluating the top ranked candidates from the previous lexical coding stage using a selected language model (Anthropic Claude or Google Gemini, selected at runtime), via a few shot prompt. The few shot prompt provides the definition of the rings, examples of high/medium/low exemplars, negatives included, therefore, the boundary is learned as opposed to just the target. Few shot prompting has been proven to improve task adherence without fine tuning the model (Brown et al., "Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners", NeurIPS 33 (2020)). The language model must produce strict JSON: relevance score, rings matched, thematic tags, two sentence annotation, verbatim citable line. If malformed output is returned from the model, the pipeline will retry once under stricter instructions; if malformed output is returned again, a zero tagged classification_failed will be recorded; if a quota or network error occurs the pipeline will fall back to lexical score thus ensuring a verdict will always be produced. When that fallback occurs the item is tagged for it (keyword_fallback or classification_error_fallback) and the digest header reports the classifier as keyword-fallback rather than naming the model, so that a reader can always tell an LLM-written annotation from a raw lexical one. Because a language model can paraphrase a sentence and still present it as a quotation, the verbatim citable line is accepted only when it appears, character for character after whitespace and quotation marks are normalized, within the source text; a line that fails this check is discarded and the deterministic extractor supplies the quote instead. The few-shot prompt and this substring verification together bound the model's output, but they do not remove the need for human-in-the-loop review, and the digest annotations are meant to be verified by a reader before they are relied upon.


## VI. Scoring, Calibration & Validation

### A. Calibration decisions
A ring must demonstrate itself as worthy of counting as present. A ring counts as present once its lexical subtotal reaches a floor of twelve — meaning it would need to make a meaningful hit on one or more words rather than merely lightly grazing a single word. This was done to avoid potential false positives where, for example, a mining claim mentions an investment, but then is incorrectly identified by the system as a theme. In other terms, the decision to apply a minimum term frequency cut-off (Manning et al. 2008) was deliberate. The threshold for the digest was set at forty, twenty fewer than the original number. Neither number is arbitrary; both were chosen against the actual score distribution the deterministic scorer produces. When the scorer is run over substantive text, on-theme and off-theme cases separate into two clusters with a wide empty band between them. Labelled off-theme texts — the negative exemplars together with a sample of live, out-of-sample listings — score between zero and eight, with a mean below one, while labelled on-theme texts score between forty and seventy-five. No case in either set lands in the nine-to-thirty-nine range. The threshold of forty therefore sits at the floor of the on-theme cluster, and the ring-present floor of twelve sits in the dead band well above the off-theme noise, so both separate signal from noise by observation rather than by intuition.

An item that clears the threshold is not the product of an adjudication; the instrument generates leads. Because the theme is narrow, many weeks produce no qualifying development, and an empty report can train a reader to stop opening it. The instrument balances these concerns with a deliberate floor rather than with indiscriminate padding. Every item at or above the threshold of forty is reported as a match, with no upper limit. When fewer than six matches exist, the digest is filled toward a minimum of six with the closest near-misses, but only those at or above a relevance floor of twenty-five; an item below that floor is never surfaced. A near-miss carried in this way is labelled a watch-list lead rather than a match, so that no reader mistakes a lead for a holding. A week in which nothing clears the floor is reported honestly, as a single sentence recording that no thematically relevant development was found and how many candidates were screened, in preference to filling the digest with irrelevant material.

### B. Validation
The author checked the lexical scoring mechanism at two levels; neither rises to validation in a strong sense, and the language throughout should be read accordingly. First, as a face-validity check, the scorer was run against the seven labelled examples in fingerprint.yaml (tests/test_pipeline.py) and reproduced the intended band on all seven, the three negatives included; the three seed awards likewise scored medium to high on their own text. This confirms only that the instrument recognizes the corpus that generated it (Saldana; Linos & Carlson), and a face-validity check on a handful of cases is, by itself, anecdotal.

Second, and to probe the more serious question of whether the fingerprint had merely memorized the three seeds or had captured a generalizable structure, the author ran a small exploratory holdout — twenty items, only four of them on-theme positives, skewed toward NAFTA and ICSID denial-of-justice awards — and scored it with scripts/eval_holdout.py. The holdout pairs four on-theme awards that played no part in development — Loewen v. United States, Mondev v. United States, Apotex v. United States, and Philip Morris v. Uruguay — against sixteen real listings drawn live from the sources; it should be read as a small exploratory check, not a validation study. At the digest threshold of forty the instrument returned a precision of 1.00, a recall of 0.75, and an accuracy of 0.95 (F1 of 0.86) on this set: every off-theme listing was correctly rejected, and three of the four unseen on-theme awards (Loewen, Mondev, and Philip Morris v. Uruguay) were correctly flagged. These figures describe twenty items with four positives and should not be read as a general performance claim; the F1 of 0.86 in particular is illustrative, not conclusive. Loewen and Mondev, moreover, exercise the judicial-measure / denial-of-justice ring far more than the full IP-as-investment intersection the theme targets, so the holdout exercises Ring 2 more independently than Ring 1. The single miss, Apotex, is instructive rather than incidental, because that case turned on a holding that the claimant's regulatory rights were not a covered investment, and the deterministic scorer under-detects cases that hinge on the negative finding of non-investment — which is precisely the paraphrase-sensitive reasoning the second, language-model stage exists to supply. That points to a tuning direction rather than a dead end: because negative-threshold rulings matter to the research question as much as successful assertions do, the paraphrase-tolerant language-model stage is where they are meant to be recovered, and adding explicit non-investment phrasings ("not a covered investment") to the fingerprint would let the lexical stage flag such rejections as well. That three of four unseen cases register at all, on their own distinct vocabulary, is suggestive that the fingerprint reaches beyond its development corpus, but on a set this small it is no more than suggestive. The holdout remains small, and the limitations in Part IX state plainly what it does not yet establish.


## VII. The Reporting Standard — The Digest as Annotated Bibliography
The digest is organized as an annotated bibliography since the reporting standards call for an interpretive description (of the law) instead of just descriptive descriptions. Each entry will follow the format of an annotated bibliography (citation + descriptive/evaluative annotations), adhering to the AALL Law Library Journal guidelines for length (50-200 words). All citations are formatted according to The Chicago Manual of Style. Additionally, all entries include a URL to assist readers with locating and accessing sources cited. Entries consistently evaluate the relevance of each item listed; specifically whether this development has implications for this project, and if so, how. All entries provide the source name(s), link to the original document/source and quote a direct, verbatim quote from that source. It provides the user with enough information to access the source (or backsource) without having to search elsewhere. If there is a limit to accessing a particular source due to being behind a firewall/paywall or limited only to metadata (such as title, authors, etc.), that limitation is noted rather than concealed.

### The Interpretive Companion — The Research Council & the Research Brief
Beyond the digest, the instrument produces a downstream interpretive companion, the ISDS Research Brief, generated in the same cycle by a small council of role-bounded stages, each trained to its task by a dedicated instruction prompt or a deterministic check: a chairman that sets the week's agenda from the research threads carried forward, an analyst that interprets the screened developments against the research question, a security officer that verifies the analyst's work against a binding calibration checklist, a deterministic integrity check that machine-verifies every cited source, and an editor that issues the brief. The analyst may consult web search, but only to deepen insight on the already-screened developments and to continue synthesizing research around the research question — not as an additional collection channel, and not to introduce material unconnected to a screened item or to the question. A binding calibration checklist, adapted from a documented pre-response self-awareness framework and enforced by the security officer, guards each issue against the characteristic language-model failure modes — fabricated citations or quotations, overreach beyond the evidence, and inflated relevance — consistent with the anti-inflation discipline already applied to the digest. Under that checklist, each high-risk element — a citation, quotation, holding, date, or figure — is to be verified against a real source before it is stated, or else marked unavailable rather than asserted; this pre-publication verification is the anti-hallucination workflow; the security officer is its gate, and a deterministic citation check its backstop — run over the brief's sources and, on demand, over this memo's own citations. That backstop is not the model auditing itself: it is a model-free script that extracts every http(s) URL from the brief and actually fetches each one over the network, marking any that fail to resolve as a suspected fabrication, while URL-less authorities — law-review articles, treatises, and awards cited by case number — are flagged for human verification rather than silently trusted. A separate analytics function tracks which catalogue sources prove receptive to the thematic intersection — quantified as each source's hit rate, the share of its screened candidates that surface as on-theme developments in the digest — so that coverage can be tuned over time toward the feeds that yield genuinely on-theme developments. The full council meets daily — the chairman sets the agenda, the analyst researches and the security officer vets, the analytics officer relates the findings to that week's screened items, and the council advances the research question and records any system or method improvement — while the weekly reconvene produces the interpretive brief and the chairman's candid accountability assessment, surfacing to the principal any matter requiring attention. So that the research compounds rather than restarting each day, the analyst maintains a living state-of-the-answer synthesis — the project's cumulative, source-anchored best answer to the research question — alongside an append-only, deduplicated insight ledger against which each session measures whether a finding is genuinely new, reporting a quiet day honestly as such rather than padding the record. A recurring human-review checkpoint spot-audits a sample of the period's cited claims and logs the result, making operational the standard that the brief's findings are leads to be verified before they are relied upon. The brief, the council's deliberation, and that accountability record are archived alongside each digest.


## VIII. Reproducibility & State
Every item processed is stored (source and unique id) in a state file called state/seen.json so no item is ever classified or reported twice — the process can recover from any corruption; the store will start fresh if there has been corruption but never shut-down the entire pipeline. The results of each scheduled run are saved to a version-controlled repository which includes both the dated digests for archiving and a snapshot of the current state, thus providing a complete record of all observations made at their respective times and scores of how they were classified — essentially an analog of preserving the original manual annotation as the ultimate source-of-truth.


## IX. Limitations
There are significant limitations of this project regarding access and coverage. Rather than hide or remove these limitations, the project has decided to state them openly so that the user knows how much data they will be able to view and/or utilize. Construct Validity. Although the theme was developed from three awards there could be underestimation of the number of doctrinal variations created by the seeds. The Theme-Extension Terms were assigned light weight values to include adjacent doctrines ("Denial of Benefits", "Substantial Business Activity") which mitigates some of the potential problem but does not resolve it completely. A most-similar-case design built on three seeds also invites the charge of overfitting, and the small exploratory holdout in Part VI.B — twenty items, only four of them positives — is the partial response to it: three of four awards the instrument had never seen were correctly identified, which is suggestive that the schema reaches some structure rather than only the seeds' idiosyncratic wording, though a set this small cannot settle the question. The residual risk is nonetheless real. Four confirmatory cases cannot exhaust the structure's linguistic expression; the holdout still skews toward NAFTA and ICSID denial-of-justice awards (Loewen and Mondev in particular exercise the judicial-measure / denial-of-justice ring more than the full IP-as-investment intersection), which means Ring 1 (IP as a protected investment) is exercised far less independently than Rings 2 and 3, so a pure IP-as-investment case carrying no judicial-measure component is the instrument's most likely false negative; and validation across non-English and non-NAFTA awards, on a larger human-coded sample, remains warranted before any claim stronger than face-plus-holdout validity is made. Two implementation simplifications belong here for the same reason: the lexical matcher operates on case-insensitive substrings and a handful of truncated stems rather than on full lemmatization, so an unusual inflection of a trigger term can be missed; and de-duplication is performed per source by stable id, so a single dispute arriving through several different feeds is only reconciled downstream at the Research Brief's insight ledger rather than merged at intake. Coverage is a Floor. Content can be denied by Paywalls, robots.txt, and because Google News RSS is currently disabled/blocked and therefore not active, the Tool only presents the minimum threshold of what can exist and does not provide a complete census of existing information. Headline-only sources, IAReporter in particular, under-score genuinely on-theme cases whenever the dispositive detail sits in the paywalled body the instrument cannot read, so those items surface as watch-list leads rather than matches. The interpretive Research Brief carries a further, distinct risk: it relies on model-generated interpretation and, through bounded web search, on material beyond the curated corpus, both of which can hallucinate citations or overstate holdings; this risk is mitigated — not eliminated — by the council's verification stage and the deliberately constrained use of search, and the brief's interpretations and supplemental findings are accordingly offered as research leads to be verified before they are relied upon, not as adjudications.
