### 3. Your central question — the bracket

**Was the design defective? Yes, three ways, and the second is worse than the one you asked about.**

**(i) Scope mismatch.** A bracket controls for the availability of the resource it brackets. Across hosts it controls only for what is common and host-invariant: that the runner has egress, that the client is the pinned build, that the process reached the end of the batch. All three are already covered — by the control row, by naming `scripts/fetch_relay.py@7fbbabf`, and by `requested`/`completed`/row count. It controls for **nothing** host-local: DNS/TLS to host B, host B's edge policy, its rate limiting, its availability. Those are the only things a bracket exists to exclude. A cross-host bracket is not a weak control for the hypothesis at issue; it is a control for a hypothesis nobody had.

**(ii) The bracket could not have worked even same-host, because the artifact has no clock.** In `fetch_relay.py` at the pinned commit, `fetched_at` is `datetime.now(timezone.utc).isoformat()` inside the manifest dict built **after** the loop — one batch-level completion instant, no per-row timestamps. The pre-registration's own words — *"the host answered normally across the window in which rows 2 and 3 were fired"* — name a window the record cannot exhibit. A passing bracket would have "established" it anyway. That is the defect I would fix first: **a design whose success is unfalsifiable is worse than one whose failure is.** A bracket can establish **order** (array index). It cannot establish **duration**.

**(iii) Your premise about the control is half-true.** `records = [fetch_one(CONTROL_URL)]` runs once, before any target. `control_ok` is an **opening sample** of runner health, not a bracket of it. So the runner-wide health you take as already controlled is controlled at the batch's first instant only — which is the very gap the bracket was invented to fill, and the one place a *host-neutral* bracket would be legitimate.

**The correct gate, prospectively.** G1 **runner gate**, batch-wide: the control row, upgraded to a control *pair* (fired first and last) — that is the one bracket that may legitimately gate rows on other hosts, because `example.com` makes no claim about any research host. G2 **host gate**, per host: a bracket gates only rows on its own host. G3 **declared edges**: each row names the gates it is conditional on; void propagates along declared edges only, never by batch membership. G4 **no "window" language** until the schema carries a per-row timestamp; until then, "ordered before/after" and nothing more.

**Why P0 is strictly dominated, in numbers.** The gate host had a perfect observed record — 23 for 23 across 13 instants and 13 runner sessions — and fired on its first outing. **No observed reliability bounds tomorrow's edge policy.** Coupling makes batch informativeness the product of every gate host's reliability while the gate's diagnostic reach stays at one host: today it converted a single-host event into the loss of four rows, two of which returned `ok`.

**Today's rule versus tomorrow's, sharply.** Today: **void, entire.** I endorse your holding without qualification, and I will name the cost so it is on the record rather than implied — under your permissive reading D1 *would* have been satisfied (row 2 returned exactly 832338 / `ca87a113c024605c…`) and C-1 too. The day's forgone purchase is one instant of UNCITRAL digest stability and one ~24-hour robots stability point. Both are cheap and both re-acquirable tomorrow. That is the whole price of holding the line, and it is far below the price of establishing that a pre-registration may be re-read once its numbers are visible. The cross-host argument is **correct on the merits and inadmissible today**; it becomes tomorrow's rule by being written into tomorrow's pre-registration before results exist, not by being applied to this one.
