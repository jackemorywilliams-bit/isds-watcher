### B. Thread T04 — Hela Schwarz: New Ring 3 Mechanism Under the China-Germany BIT (2003)

#### B.1 First decided ICSID case under the China-Germany BIT (2003): Hela Schwarz GmbH v. China

The most significant new finding today is the **Hela Schwarz GmbH v. People's Republic of China** (ICSID Case No. ARB/17/19) final award, dated **December 10, 2025**, published January 15, 2026.

- **Claimant:** Hela Schwarz GmbH (German food and spice manufacturer)
- **Respondent:** People's Republic of China
- **Treaty basis:** China-Germany BIT (2003) — the treaty our research identified as the top Ring 1 vehicle for pharmaceutical trade secrets ("trade and business secrets" in the investment definition)
- **Subject matter:** Expropriation of land use rights held by the claimant's Chinese subsidiary (Ji'nan Hela Schwarz Food Co., Ltd, "JHSF") during a mixed-use redevelopment project in Shandong province. Claim value: EUR 90+ million
- **Outcome:** Tribunal dismissed all claims on **both jurisdiction and merits grounds**
- **Counsel for China:** A&O Shearman and the Ministry of Commerce
- **Tribunal:** Sir Daniel Bethlehem KC (Presiding Arbitrator), Professor Campbell McLachlan KC, Mr. Roland Ziadé

Sources: [(A&O Shearman, "Landmark ICSID Victory")](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/news/landmark-icsid-victory-for-the-peoples-republic-of-china); [(italaw, Hela Schwarz)](https://www.italaw.com/cases/5973); [(UNCTAD Navigator, Hela Schwarz v. China)](https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/investment-dispute-settlement/cases/805/hela-schwarz-v-china); [(CDR News, "Court Withdrawal Conundrum")](https://www.cdr-news.com/categories/arbitration-adr/icsid-tribunal-tackles-court-withdrawal-conundrum/)

#### B.2 The jurisdictional ground: concurrent proceedings / court-withdrawal requirement

The China-Germany BIT (2003) contains a provision — the specific article number is not confirmed from open sources, but referred to in academic literature as the concurrent proceedings clause — that requires an investor to withdraw domestic court proceedings before the ICSID arbitration can proceed. In Hela Schwarz, JHSF had filed **the same claim before Chinese courts** before Hela Schwarz filed the ICSID claim. The tribunal held that the ICSID arbitration was **invalid** because the claimant failed to withdraw the domestic court proceedings. The claim was dismissed on this jurisdictional ground (and also on the merits).

The CDR News characterised this as the "court withdrawal conundrum" — a structural feature of many older Chinese BITs that requires claimants to choose between domestic and international forums (a variant of a fork-in-the-road / concurrent-proceedings restriction), but that the Hela Schwarz case has now clarified applies to Chinese BIT arbitration.

Academic context: "Parallel Proceedings under Chinese BITs: The Case of Hela Schwarz GmbH v PR China" (Journal of International Dispute Settlement, 2020, [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jids/article-abstract/11/2/335/5807886)) and "Parallel Proceedings under Chinese BITs" (Chapter 7 of *Decoding Chinese Bilateral Investment Treaties*, Cambridge University Press) both address this structural issue. The Hela Schwarz final award resolves the question: the parallel-proceedings/court-withdrawal requirement is jurisdictional and not merely procedural; failure to withdraw domestic proceedings bars ICSID jurisdiction.

**Calibration note:** The specific BIT article number is not confirmed from open sources (the treaty PDF is 403-blocked). The holding itself — dismissal for failure to withdraw domestic Chinese court proceedings — is confirmed from CDR News, A&O Shearman, and UNCTAD Navigator at high confidence. The characterisation of the precise mechanism as "concurrent proceedings/court-withdrawal" rather than a strict fork-in-the-road clause is drawn from search summaries; the exact doctrinal label awaits full award access. Mark the dismissal itself as HIGH confidence; the specific treaty provision and exact doctrine as MEDIUM confidence pending full award access.

#### B.3 Significance for the China pharmaceutical trade-secrets scenario (T04)

This finding adds a **third Ring 3 mechanism** applicable to the China BIT pharmaceutical ISDS scenario, distinct from the two Ring 3 mechanisms already documented:

1. Philip Morris / critical-date doctrine (applicable when investor restructures to gain treaty access)
2. NAFTA Art. 1121 waiver consistency (applicable in NAFTA/CUSMA cases — not the China BIT context)
3. **China-Germany BIT concurrent-proceedings / court-withdrawal requirement (Hela Schwarz, Dec. 2025 — NOW CONFIRMED):** an investor seeking to challenge China's pharmaceutical data regime under the China-Germany BIT (2003) must ensure it has not filed, or has withdrawn, any concurrent claims before Chinese regulatory bodies or courts before commencing ICSID arbitration. A multinational pharmaceutical originator that had first pursued Chinese administrative appeals of an NMPA data protection decision — a natural first step — may be procedurally foreclosed from ICSID arbitration under this BIT if those proceedings are not withdrawn.

This is not merely a tactical warning: it fundamentally shapes what a pharmaceutical investor would need to do before filing. In the China pharmaceutical data scenario (NMPA denies or restricts data exclusivity protection; European investor challenges under the China-Germany BIT), the investor would face a Ring 3 threshold question of parallel proceedings *before* the Ring 1 / Ring 2 merits are ever reached.

### C. Thread T04 — China Pharmaceutical Data Gap: Clarification

#### C.1 The gap is at the State Council level, persists through final measures

The USTR 2026 Special 301 Report's "unfair and discriminatory" characterisation specifically identifies that the **State Council Implementing Regulations of the Drug Administration Law** (issued January 2026, effective May 2026) "did not address what period of protection should apply with respect to drugs first approved outside of China." The NMPA Implementation Measures (May 15, 2026) removed the draft's "time-gap reduction" rule (awarding full six-year protection to drugs first marketed overseas upon their China approval), but the higher-level statutory gap — the failure to define the protection period for drugs whose marketing approval was first sought outside China — persists in the implementing regulations. The US and China agreed to address this in future negotiations. Sources: [(National Law Review, May 2026)](https://natlawreview.com/article/china-retains-priority-watch-list-status-ustrs-2026-special-301-report-continuing); [(China IP Law Update, May 2026)](https://www.chinaiplawupdate.com/2026/05/china-retains-priority-watch-list-status-in-ustrs-2026-special-301-report-with-continuing-concerns-over-technology-transfer-trade-secrets-and-counterfeiting/); [(Spruson & Ferguson, "Data exclusivity comes into effect")](https://www.spruson.com/china-data-exclusivity-comes-into-effect/); [(PhRMA, USTR 2026 Special 301 blog)](https://phrma.org/blog/ustr-s-2026-special-301-report-stands-up-for-american-innovation/)

**Significance:** The two-layer characterisation in STATE_OF_THE_ANSWER.md is correct. The NMPA measures provide the detailed protection framework but the gap on "drugs first approved outside China" exists at the State Council regulatory level — this is the harder gap to fill because it requires either further legislation or a negotiated bilateral commitment. The USTR characterisation is calibrated correctly: it targets the State Council Implementing Regulations' silence on this point, which is the gap that would disadvantage multinational originator pharmaceutical companies (who typically seek first approval in their home country before China).
