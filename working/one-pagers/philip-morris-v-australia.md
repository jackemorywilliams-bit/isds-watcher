## I. Case Record

**[SOURCE FACT]** Philip Morris Asia Limited v. The Commonwealth of Australia, PCA Case No. 2012-12, Award on Jurisdiction and Admissibility (Perm. Ct. Arb. Dec. 17, 2015) (Böckstiegel, P.; Kaufmann-Kohler; McRae) arose from Australia's enactment of the Tobacco Plain Packaging Act 2011 and its implementing Tobacco Plain Packaging Regulations 2011 — together the Plain Packaging Measures — and was brought under the Agreement between the Government of Hong Kong and the Government of Australia for the Promotion and Protection of Investments of 15 September 1993, applying the 2010 UNCITRAL Rules (¶¶ 5, 13, 16–17). The claimant, PM Asia, owned 100% of PM Australia, which owned 100% of PML, an Australian trading company holding rights to intellectual property including "registered and unregistered trademarks", copyrights, and designs (¶ 6). PM Asia alleged the legislation barred use of that intellectual property on tobacco packaging and sought suspension of enforcement or compensation "of the order of billions of Australian dollars" (¶¶ 7–8). The restructuring placing the Australian subsidiaries under PM Asia was decided on 3 September 2010 and completed by share transfer on 23 February 2011; the TPP Act passed Parliament on 21 November 2011 — the same date as the Notice of Arbitration — and received Royal Assent on 1 December 2011 (¶¶ 164, 176–177, 533; ¶ 13). The proceedings were bifurcated, with two preliminary objections heard first (¶ 9). The Tribunal held that PM Asia had not shown pre-restructuring control over the Australian investments under Article 1(e) of the BIT (¶ 509); that the investment was admitted under Australian law, so "the Respondent’s non-admission" "objection must thus fail" (¶ 523); and that "the requirements for the Tribunal’s jurisdiction ratione temporis are met" (¶ 534). It nonetheless found an abuse of rights: commencing arbitration is abusive when an investor restructures to gain the "protection of an investment treaty at a point in time when a specific dispute was foreseeable" (¶ 554), foreseeability meaning a "reasonable prospect that a measure that may give rise to a treaty claim will materialise" (¶ 585). Because the Plain Packaging Measures were foreseeable from the 29 April 2010 governmental announcement and the restructuring's principal, if not sole, purpose was Treaty protection, "the claims raised in this arbitration are inadmissible" and "the Tribunal is precluded from exercising jurisdiction over this dispute" (¶¶ 569, 584, 586–588). The award is interim — final save as to costs (¶¶ 10, 589).

## II. Three-Ring Read

**[FRAMEWORK ANALYSIS]** Ring 1 (IP as covered investment): the asserted investment was shares in the Australian holding chain whose value, on the claimant's own account, rested on PML's intellectual property — trademarks, copyright, designs, and get-up (¶ 6). The Tribunal never tested whether that IP qualified as a covered investment on the merits; the only investment-definition work done was the Article 1(e) control inquiry, which PM Asia lost as to the pre-restructuring period (¶¶ 508–509). Ring 2 (challenged measure): a general legislative public-health measure — the TPP Act and Regulations — challenged as destroying branded-product value (¶¶ 5, 7). Ring 3 (jurisdictional disposal): the case died entirely at the gate, but on abuse-of-rights admissibility grounds tied to the timing and purpose of the restructuring, not on any holding about the IP itself. The Tribunal expressly rejected the non-admission and ratione temporis objections (¶¶ 523, 534) before disposing of the claims on abuse of rights (¶ 588), so the gate that closed was the treaty-shopping gate, not the IP-as-investment gate.

## III. What the Award Does and Does Not Establish

**[RESEARCH-QUESTION IMPLICATION]** For the question of when IP asserted as a covered investment and challenged via a state measure is disposed of at the jurisdictional gate, this award establishes the timing-and-purpose pathway: a foreseeability-based abuse-of-rights test (¶¶ 554, 585) that lets a tribunal dismiss an IP-centered claim without ever characterizing the IP. It does not reach whether trademarks or get-up are themselves protected investments under the BIT; whether plain packaging amounted to expropriation or breached any substantive standard; or how a tribunal would treat an identical claim brought by an entity that held the investment before the measure became foreseeable. The merits of the IP claim were never adjudicated (¶¶ 9, 588–590). The case therefore supports the proposition that jurisdictional disposal of IP claims can be entirely orthogonal to the IP — a lesson about claimant structure and timing, not about the status of intangibles as investments.
