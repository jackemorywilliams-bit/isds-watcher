not a fact about the source. So **the last whitespace-delimited token of any capped excerpt is

Two honest limits on this item, stated because the rule is weaker than it sounds. First, **it is a

## 7. Method note for the self-training file

**(a) The rule adopted 2026-08-15 is right in substance and wrong in its cut. Refine it.** Yesterday's lesson read: *"for a person, catalogue means a work-record, not a profile page. A profile page's title carries affiliation and stops there."* Today falsified the second sentence twice. `Paul Sharp - Partner, Deals - Valuations, Modelling and Disputes - PwC Canada | LinkedIn` is a **profile page** whose title carries the practice group; `Douglas Uffen, P. Geoph. (APEGA)` is a **profile page** whose title carries a professional designation. **The real variable is not profile-vs-work-record. It is whether the host's TITLE TEMPLATE has a role-or-field slot.** Three templates, now observed:

- **University profile** → `Name | Institution`. Carries affiliation, stops. *(Bankes, 2026-08-15.)*
- **LinkedIn** → `Name - Headline - Employer | LinkedIn`, and the headline slot is the practice group. **Carries the field.**
- **Own-firm / credential page** → may carry a protected designation (`P. Geoph. (APEGA)`). **Carries the field.**

**Operational form: before spending a call for a person's field, ask which host's title template has a field slot, and aim at that host.** Work-records remain excellent; they are no longer the only instrument.

**(b) The bare exact-phrase name was the best call of the session, and it was the cheapest.** `"Doug Uffen"`, with **nothing else**, returned five distinct pages, two title-carried fields and a collision warning. `0815-O10` treats the bare surname as a floor for declaring absence; **today it was also the ceiling for retrieval.** For an uncommon name, **added terms subtract**: they filter a small result set that did not need filtering. Reserve keyword scaffolding for common names.

**(c) For a common name, the discriminator is firm-token plus ROLE-noun, and the colloquial firm token beat the legal one.** Query 1 used `PricewaterhouseCoopers` with subject nouns and returned the *firm* (a GAR organisation page), the *practice* (Lexology ranking tables) and an unrelated PDF — **no person**. Query 3 used `PwC` plus `partner` plus `expert witness` and returned the person twice, first-party and platform. **Firms are indexed under the name people type; persons are indexed under their role word.** Yesterday's query-2 post-mortem said "for a common surname, the affiliation, not the subject, is the discriminator" — today sharpens it: **affiliation *and a role noun*; and use the short form of the firm.**

**(d) The generated summary was substantively excellent and had to be refused anyway, three times.** Query 1's summary offered a date, a responding firm and an engaging party — a complete answer. The title carried none of it. **The moment to notice `0811-B4` is when the summary is good, because that is the only moment it is tempting.** I record that I felt the pull and did not act on it.

**(e) Never pipe a novelty screen into `head`.** My first Screen 2 would have reported both target names absent from a record that holds them fifteen and fourteen times, because a high-frequency co-term (`Einarsson`) ate the window. **A screen must be run per-designator with a derived count, never as one grep of many terms through a truncating pipe.** Proposed as a standing rule.

**(f) When a search result set is thin, read the instrument's source code.** The session's only body-carried finding (E7) came from `scripts/fetch_relay.py`, not from the web. **On a fetch-dead day the repository is still a live, fully readable corpus, and the code that produced the record's own strings is evidence about those strings.**
