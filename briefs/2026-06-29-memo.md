**(a) Disposition of A/CN.9/1246 — must not be logged as "adopted."** The document is real and was retrieved: A/CN.9/1246 is the "Draft supplementary provisions on the conduct of proceedings to resolve international investment disputes (advance copy)," listed among the texts to be presented to the Commission, and the advance copy itself carries the UN General Assembly distribution header "Distr.: Limited, 15 April 2026." Its disposition track is the MIIR-Protocol / UAR-supplement structure: it would be possible to make the supplementary provisions applicable to investment disputes under existing treaties through a mechanism akin to the Transparency Convention, with the MIIR being prepared by the Working Group aiming to provide such a mechanism.

Critically — and this resolves the verification flag — **the 59th session opens on the week-ending date itself:** the 59th session runs 29 June – 10 July 2026 in New York. The Commission landing page already lists the *inputs* for that session, including A/CN.9/1246 and A/CN.9/1269, the compilation of comments on the draft supplementary provisions. No post-session report (no A/CN.9/-series report of the 59th session) exists yet, because the session has not concluded. **Therefore: log A/CN.9/1246 as "submitted to the Commission as an advance copy, with a compilation of comments (A/CN.9/1269); disposition pending."** Any secondary source claiming "adoption" this week would be reporting a future or an inference — reject it per the agenda. What the pre-session record supports is only that the package was engineered to *reach* the Commission cleanly: by bundling the draft provisions on the conduct of ISDS proceedings into a single package, States mapped out a path to present a concrete deliverable to the Commission in July 2026, sidestepping prolonged debates on optionality and selective adoption. "Deliverable-ready" is not "adopted."

**(b) Did the dual-nationality / natural-person gap stay off-package?** Yes — confirmed by the *contents* of the package, not by inference. A/CN.9/1246 is the procedural-conduct instrument (evidence, interim measures, early dismissal, costs, TPF, counterclaims); the cross-cutting and substantive-jurisdiction items sit on separate, less mature tracks. IISD's review of the immediately preceding sessions records that the 53rd session addressed procedural and cross-cutting issues with mixed results — a broad scope for counterclaims and a TPF-disclosure compromise — while progress on cost allocation was slow and discussions on the calculation of damages were deferred; the Working Group confirmed the procedural and cross-cutting provisions will form a Protocol to the Reform Package, and the 54th session turned to the standing-tribunal and appellate statutes. Nationality-planning / denial-of-benefits (the *Jason Yu Song v China* natural-person gap from our prior weeks) appears in none of these tracks. **The gap remained off-package.** This is the most important takeaway for our Ring 3 question: the reform deliverable most likely to be advanced in 2026 does *not* touch the abuse-of-right / treaty-shopping / nationality-planning doctrines that dispose of our seed cases. The jurisdictional gate the project studies stays where it is — judge-made, case-by-case — even as the surrounding procedural furniture is rebuilt.

## 3. New, sourced insight (one, and only one)
Beyond confirming gap-status, the contents review surfaces a genuinely new structural point not in the prior record: the package's procedural reforms run on an **opt-in, non-retroactive** architecture. As with the 2013 Transparency Rules, the new supplement would not automatically apply to ISDS proceedings arising under pre-existing investment treaties, allowing States that oppose it to avoid its application — no State that opposes any of the reforms would be bound, whether through revision of the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules or through their application as treaty provisions in a Protocol to the MIIR. For the project this means: even if A/CN.9/1246's early-dismissal/manifest-lack-of-merit provisions were adopted, they would **not** retro-apply to the BIT-era treaties under which IP-as-investment claims (Philip Morris, Eli Lilly, Bridgestone, Hela Schwarz) arise. The doctrinal tools that dispose of our cases at the gate will, for the foreseeable future, remain treaty-and-tribunal-specific rather than harmonised by the reform. That is the durable answer to "how does the reform bear on Ring 3": **largely not, for the legacy treaty stock that generates IP-investment disputes.**
