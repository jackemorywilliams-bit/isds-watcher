Research Methodology Memorandum

The ISDS Thematic Watcher

TO Dr. Ximena Benavides, Department of Legal Studies, Terry College of Business
FROM Emory Williams, Undergraduate Research Assistant
DATE 06/08/2026
RE Methodology Memo — The ISDS Thematic Watcher: design, justification, and preliminary validation of an automated lead-generation and annotated-bibliography instrument for the IP-as-investment, judicial-measure, and jurisdictional-admissibility intersection

I. Research Question & Scope

This memo sets out the operational methodology of the ISDS Thematic Watcher — an automated monitoring tool I built for this project using Claude AI and Claude Code as the development medium. Every operational choice in its development was grounded in the literature reviewed to date. Beyond documenting the methodology, the memo speaks to one of the project's substantive questions: How can changes in investor-State dispute settlement practice be identified, over time and within a particular area of law, where an intellectual property right is asserted as a covered investment, a governmental act or court decision is challenged as a violation of that right, and a jurisdictional or admissibility doctrine is live and litigated in the case, whether or not it determines that the case cannot proceed — and how can that be done repeatedly and uniformly? Rather than resolve the substance of those issues — whether trade secrets and clinical trial data qualify as protected investments — this memo addresses the methodological approach used to identify the areas of law that meet those criteria; for the substantive arguments on what rights are entitled to protection under international investment agreements, the Ferguson and Kim memos should be consulted. What follows is how the ISDS Thematic Watcher turns that legal framework into a repeatable process with multiple points of auditability, including contestation where necessary. The memo proceeds through the theoretical basis of the instrument (Part II), its source architecture and sourcing mechanism (Part III), candidate identification and enrichment (Part IV), a two-stage classification cascade (Part V), scoring, calibration, and validation (Part VI), the reporting standard for the digest (Part VII), the interpretive companion produced alongside it (Part VIII), reproducibility (Part IX), and a candid account of limitations (Part X).

II. Theoretical Framework — The Three-Ring Thematic Fingerprint

A. Why a thematic instrument, and not a keyword search

What I am watching for is not one specific word or phrase. A relationship among three doctrines cannot be reduced to one keyword; each time you try, you lose the essence of the idea. In one case a court may reference the term “patent” a hundred times in the briefing and yet have no relation to my claim; conversely, a court may never once say “abuse of right,” and the case still fits the category exactly. So as much as this looked like a lexical problem, it was really an interpretive one, and that is where qualitative method comes in: how do you develop a tool to identify a category when there are only three examples to work with? Treating the fingerprint's development as an exercise in theory-informed sampling and most-similar-case design — which Linos & Carlson urge on legal scholars, since legal authors tend to pick examples unsystematically and analyze them in purely doctrinal terms (Linos & Carlson, Qualitative Methods for Law Review Writing, 84 U. Chi. L. Rev. 213 (2017)) — I did not build the seed corpus to represent all of ISDS. I built the seed corpus out of structural similarity along the dimensions I care about — the logic of a most-similar-case method.

B. The seed corpus

The seed corpus is comprised of three awards. Each award arranges the same three elements of the structure in a distinctly different way: a claimed intellectual property right alleged to be a covered investment; a challenged governmental action or judicial decision alleged to have damaged that investment; and a rule applied at the jurisdictional, admissibility, or merits stage that determined the outcome. In this way each award presents a unique disposition of the same structure. Philip Morris Asia Ltd. v. Commonwealth of Australia, PCA Case No. 2012-12, Award on Jurisdiction and Admissibility (December 17, 2015). In this case, Philip Morris Asia had brought a claim against the Commonwealth of Australia over the Tobacco Plain Packaging Act, a law that prohibited the use of trademarked names and logos on cigarette packaging. Philip Morris claimed that this barred it from using its intellectual property on tobacco products and packaging, damaging the value of its investments. The Tribunal did not reach the merits of the case. Instead, it held the claims inadmissible as an abuse of rights. The reason was that Philip Morris had made corporate changes to its organizational structure, placing the Australian subsidiaries under the claimant, at a time when the present dispute was reasonably foreseeable. The Tribunal found that these changes were made principally, if not solely, to gain treaty protection for that foreseeable dispute. In Eli Lilly & Co. v. The Government of Canada, ICSID Case No. UNCT/14/2, Final Award (March 16, 2017), the Claimant argued that the Canadian courts' invalidation of its Zyprexa and Strattera patents based on the promise utility doctrine constituted an unlawful expropriation of its investments under NAFTA Article 1110 and also breached the minimum standard of treatment under NAFTA Article 1105. However, it is worth noting that at no time did the Claimant allege a denial of justice; as the Tribunal records at paragraph 129 of the Award, the Claimant conceded that there had been none. Moreover, the Tribunal declined to determine whether a judicial measure could constitute a violation of either Article 1105 or Article 1110 in the absence of a denial of justice (paragraphs 219-220). The case was decided on the merits, and the Claimant lost. The Tribunal did not find any fundamental or dramatic change in Canadian patent law. Nor did the Tribunal find anything arbitrary or discriminatory in the utility requirement as applied. Ultimately, the claim was dismissed in its entirety (Eli Lilly & Co. v. Canada, Final Award (Mar. 16, 2017), italaw case 1625, https://www.italaw.com/cases/1625; IISD, Investment Treaty News (June 12, 2017), https://www.iisd.org/itn/2017/06/12/highly-anticipated-nafta-award-rejects-patent-law-related-claim-against-canada-matthew-levine/). The claimants' allegations in Bridgestone Licensing Services, Inc. & Bridgestone Americas, Inc. v. Republic of Panama, ICSID Case No. ARB/16/34, Award (Aug. 14, 2020) included that a judgment of the Supreme Court of Panama constituted a denial of justice. The case also raised another important issue, the operating-entity versus holding-company question: whether the trademark licenses were a covered investment, a jurisdictional and standing issue of the kind examined in Philip Morris Asia Ltd. On that question, the Decision on Expedited Objections (Dec. 13, 2017) settled the threshold largely in the claimants' favor, finding the relevant licenses to be investments. The claimants then withdrew their National Treatment (Article 10.3) and Most-Favored-Nation (Article 10.4) claims in their Reply, leaving denial of justice under Article 10.5 as the sole claim to relief by the Award stage (para. 129). In reaching a conclusion on the merits of that claim, the Tribunal concluded that there was no denial of justice because the errors it found in the Supreme Court's reasoning were merely errors of judgment (para. 547), and that the claimants had failed to establish the loss they claimed (para. 570); accordingly, the claim was dismissed (para. 590) (Bridgestone v. Panama, Award (Aug. 14, 2020), italaw case 4475, https://www.italaw.com/cases/4475; Decision on Expedited Objections (Dec. 13, 2017), https://jusmundi.com/en/document/decision/en-bridgestone-americas-inc-and-bridgestone-licensing-services-inc-v-republic-of-panama-decision-on-expedited-objections-wednesday-13th-december-2017). The Index Unit for all awards is the Arbitrators' language itself, i.e. the operational phrases used were directly extracted from each award as shown by the full Per-Ring Extraction in PLAN.md. This selection was intentional and corresponds to one of the basic principles of information retrieval: The best terms for distinguishing among relevant documents will always be those appearing within those documents themselves (Salton & McGill, Introduction to Modern Information Retrieval (1983); Manning et al., Introduction to Information Retrieval (2008)).

The two basic concepts of the literature review -- the memos from Ferguson and Kim can be seen in their full form at lit-review/ -- were the basis of the research question and will serve as the base for Ring 1; however, they did not represent a fourth case, rather they complemented the three arbitration awards. In Trade Secrets at Risk, Alexander Ferguson stated that trade secrets are likely to be the most vulnerable category of intellectual property and further stated that there already exists an adequate amount of protections for trade secrets as covered investments under existing regimes when the regime is properly interpreted: he defines the asset through TRIPS Article 39 and VCLT Article 31(3)(c) and states that this protection is threatened by a four part risk taxonomy (disclosure, use, regulation, and combinations of these), and also provides a four-question indirect expropriation test -- does the state's protection have to be absolute? Has the state caused a significant interference with the legal protection of the secret, with the secret's exclusive commercial use, or a significant decrease in the commercial value of the secret? All of this is based on a recognition/enforceability gap which represents a structural issue: International law only protects that which municipal law of the host state currently recognizes. The direct counterpoint is Daria Kim’s Protecting Trade Secrets under International Investment Law: reasoning from pharmaceutical clinical trial data, she concludes that such data is unlikely to win protection through ISDS. She has four reasons for this conclusion, and a fifth observation, at her footnote 23, that goes to the forum rather than to the merits. Clinical data differs from other intellectual property assets in how it is built, and this difference appears as soon as the “investment” threshold is reached. The Apotex v. United States case defines the outer limit of what would be considered an “investment,” the point where protection ceases to exist, while Philip Morris v. Uruguay offers a discretionary jurisdictional backstop through the Salini contribution-to-development criterion. Whenever the disclosure is made in the form of non-commercial regulation, it is often difficult or even impossible to establish the counterfactual of what would have occurred had the data remained confidential; this creates a substantial evidentiary burden for the party seeking compensation under the expropriation standard. The legitimate-expectations approach via fair and equitable treatment is so vague as to provide no basis for implying confidentiality. Finally, TRIPS Article 39(3) establishes a requirement to protect against unfair commercial use, but it does not prevent a government from disclosing the data generally; because of this, a disclosure made for public-health purposes may be viewed as non-compensable under the police-powers carve-out. The fifth point is orthogonal to those four: what the investor actually wants is for the disclosure not to happen, and an investment tribunal’s ordinary remedy is compensation rather than an order restraining the State from disclosing, so the relief on offer is not the relief that would preserve the secret. The real difference between Ferguson and Kim — his optimism set against her structural skepticism about the very same treaty architecture (BITs, the ICSID Convention, TRIPS Article 39, and the VCLT) — is the core of the conceptual framework for the research question. Ring 1's working vocabulary comes from these two memoranda rather than from any single award; trade secret, clinical trial data, test data, data exclusivity, unfair commercial use, TRIPS Article 39(3). So do the doctrinal stakes that make a development worth surfacing at all.

C. The three rings

The first ring — intellectual property as a protected investment. The most significant recurring theme across the readings has been whether, and under what conditions, intellectual property can be considered a “covered investment” (Mercurio, Awakening the Sleeping Giant, 15 J. Int'l Econ. L. 871 (2012); Correa & Viñuales, Intellectual Property Rights as Protected Investments: How Open are the Gates?, 19 J. Int'l Econ. L. 91 (2016)). The first ring will be most powerful when the asset is an informational one, when ownership is divided between two entities, a holding entity and an operating entity (a condition that was true in Bridgestone), and when the asset carries the same structural vulnerabilities that the Ferguson and Kim memos identified for trade secrets and clinical trial data. Ring 1 also incorporates the four Salini criteria: contribution, duration, risk, and contribution to the host economy. The use of these criteria gives tribunals substantial latitude in their application (compare Salini Costruttori S.p.A. v. Morocco, ICSID Case No. ARB/00/4). Triggers for ring one include: covered investment; promise utility doctrine; exploitation of the trademark. Ring Two – Regulatory/Judicial Action as the Challenged Behavior (Weighted). Two of these three seed claims were based on actions taken by domestic courts as opposed to actions of a governmental agency. Bridgestone was directly claiming that there had been a denial of justice, while Eli Lilly framed its judicial-measure claim under NAFTA Articles 1105 and 1110 but never actually made an allegation of a denial of justice, a distinction the tribunal itself preserved (Eli Lilly, Final Award paras. 219-220). The standards for holding a state liable for the actions of its judiciary come from Paulsson, Denial of Justice in International Law (2005): a State can only be held liable for its courts' decisions upon a clear demonstration of manifest injustice, and mere error does not meet that standard. The seed tribunals use essentially the same terms to articulate their findings of liability — conduct that was outrageous and egregious, manifestly unjust judgments, rulings so erroneous that no reasonable or competent tribunal could have rendered them. The seed corpus depends on this ring for much of its strength. Thus, I assigned considerable weight to it, as a claimant alleging a wrongfully rendered court ruling could possibly reach MEDIUM on this ring alone. I intentionally weighted the judicial-measure ring in this way; it was a design decision and not an oversight. There is admittedly some risk in it, since an IP-as-investment case reaching the threshold through an administrative route alone would likely be under-read. This ring, however, will only ever reach MEDIUM on its own, and never HIGH; so while the weighting widens recall, no single doctrine can manufacture a top-band hit unaided, and the blind spot that remains is stated plainly in Part X. In addition to these acknowledged methods of determining categories' weights based on frequency of claims within a coding corpus (Saldaña, Coding Manual), there are several other recognized methods. Triggers for ring two include: denial of justice; manifestly unjust judgment; minimum standard of treatment. Ring Three – Threshold Questions regarding Jurisdiction and Admissibility as a Potentially Dispositive Dimension. This ring observes the doctrines a case litigates at the jurisdictional and admissibility stage, whether or not they end it: abuse of right, treaty shopping, foreseeability of the dispute, restructuring for treaty protection, and the disputed definitions of "the investor" and/or "the investment." Among the seeds, this dimension was primarily established through Philip Morris v. Australia — the only case that was decided based on it (held inadmissible as an abuse of right without addressing the merits). It played a secondary role in Bridgestone where the standing/covered investment question was answered mostly for the claimants, and thus the merits of the case determined the outcome; and it did not form part of the reasoning in Eli Lilly — which was decided on the merits. The point here is that these preliminary questions may act as dispositive issues — and did in Philip Morris — even though all three seeds were not decided based on these issues. The most recent authority used to develop the integration is Baumgartner, Treaty Shopping in International Investment Law (2016) — which expands upon an earlier abuse-of-right principle (Byers, Abuse of Rights: An Old Principle, A New Age, 47 McGill L.J. 389 (2002)) — alongside the standards provided in the leading texts (Dolzer & Schreuer, Principles of International Investment Law (2nd ed. 2012); McLachlan et al., International Investment Arbitration: Substantive Principles (2nd ed. 2017)). Triggers for ring three include: abuse of right; reasonably foreseeable; critical date; shell subsidiary.

D. The scoring grammar

The scoring grammar indicates whether a band is considered "crossed". Two rings need to be visible for a HIGH score (70+). A MEDIUM score (40-69) can be confirmed by a combination of a strong first ring and a weak second ring, or solely by the judicial measure. A LOW score (< 40) is confirmed by a weak single ring or no rings at all. These bands are scores rather than doctrinal classifications, and they should be read against the research question rather than as it: only HIGH requires two or more rings and can therefore indicate the intersection this memo is about; MEDIUM reflects one strong ring, or the judicial-measure ring alone, and is an adjacent doctrinal lead; LOW is background or off-theme. No band asserts that all three rings are present, and the instrument does not compute a direct-match, adjacent-lead, and background classification of its own. An automatic LOW is assigned for extractive disputes, sovereign debt disputes, and intra-EU energy disputes, unless evidence exists indicating that an IP or judicial angle may exist. The entire set of phrases along with their respective weights (summing to 100 per ring), reside in a machine readable format in fingerprint.yaml. In order to identify each of the phrases referenced above, they are each searched for within each field of an item -- title, summary, body text -- as a case insensitive substring rather than as a whole word. Therefore, the trigger will fire regardless of what words are immediately before and after the identified term. The fingerprint holds no truncated stems, and it does not identify variations through lemmatization or regular-expression pattern matching; a phrase is found only where it appears literally. This too is noted in Part X as one of several known simplifications.

III. Source Architecture & Research Methods

I chose a methodical approach when selecting the source materials and testing their credibility, and I strategically selected multiple different kinds of sources. Doing so let me get a wide array of information concerning investment disputes, structured around the four ways I could find such information: institutional documents, archival records, analyses, and practitioner commentary. The open repositories I identified were the ICSID docket; UNCTAD's Investment Dispute Settlement Navigator and World Investment Report; italaw's primary-document archive; IISD's Investment Treaty News; IAReporter, limited to headline level; the Permanent Court of Arbitration's press page; and Bing News search feeds, polled through eight fixed, fingerprint-derived queries with results deduplicated across queries. To acquire more than the open repositories offer, I added two further channels. The first gives access to the operator's own Google Alerts feeds, polled as RSS, which provide updates on both pending and seed case developments. The second acquires new academic literature related to the seed cases and doctrines through Google Scholar alerts, received in a designated mailbox. The amount of material accessible splits into three categories. Full read: I can view all content on the ICSID docket, UNCTAD, italaw, IISD Investment Treaty News, the PCA press page, Bing News results, and the operator's own Google Alerts and Google Scholar feeds, as the publishers allow. Headline-only read: IAReporter, which publishes only titles and leads because it keeps article bodies behind a paywall. Disabled: any individual page denied by a robots.txt rule or a login wall. This tiered access is why I view the tool as a lead-generation floor and nothing more: the minimum count of meaningful events that publicly available text can produce, never a total census of ISDS activity at the intersection. The primary motivation behind the design was triangulation — cross-checking between separate collections — on the assumption that results appearing the same way in two or more independent sources are more likely to reflect an actual pattern than a random occurrence. The repositories used by the tool are also the ones I use manually when back-sourcing; the tool just works across all of them simultaneously instead of one at a time. Collection follows predetermined extraction criteria: once relevant listings have been identified, the tool retrieves the original full-text document of the proceeding and records a reference for each item, along with the access date and time. That unique identifier is also how duplication is avoided; because the seen-state file at state/seen.json keys each processed item by its source and id, the same listing is never evaluated twice. Listings referencing the same dispute in multiple feeds are then resolved downstream, at the Research Brief's deduplicated insight ledger, rather than during intake; fully merging one dispute across feeds at the scoring stage — by matching party names, dates, and case numbers — is not yet done, and remains a recognized limitation described in Part X. On privacy and access: I have never used any information that has been placed behind a subscription-based paywall or an authentication barrier. I follow standard robot exclusion protocols. I do not send automated queries to any single domain at intervals shorter than three seconds. I also provide identifying contact information in my User-Agent header. Where a research session has no network access of its own, these same requests are issued on my behalf by the project's own GitHub Actions runner through the identical fetcher, under the same robots, interval, and User-Agent rules; the run identifier and retrieval time are recorded with the item. My one potential exception is the operator's own subscribed Google Alerts feeds, which Google provides for the operator to poll within a feed reader.

IV. Candidate Identification & Enrichment

Since a candidate's content determines how it gets identified and grouped, enrichment has to happen before classification. To enrich a candidate, I start by pulling in the candidate's original source document; removing all non-readable content from it; and analyzing each remaining sentence as to how much doctrine that particular sentence contains. My analysis weighs the number of fingerprint keywords present in the sentence (the most valuable); followed by the number of holding verbs (held, found, dismissed); followed by the number of evaluative terms (egregious, manifest, denial). Sentences which contain very little other than date references, or were written solely in uppercase letters, will be discarded. Therefore, paragraph numbers and case captions will not receive a high score. The classifier then evaluates the remainder of the readable body, and the sentence with the highest value is selected as the citable line for the candidate. Just as a properly placed pin cite allows researchers to quickly locate the referenced passage when back-sourcing a well-researched bibliography, that line provides readers at least one sentence worth reading and provides the digest with a verbatim quote to serve as an anchor.

V. Classification Procedure — A Two-Stage Cascade

There are two stages to classification, and each has its own advantages and drawbacks for precision and recall. Stage one is lexical coding – deterministic, and transparent. All candidates are ranked based on the keyword weights contained within the fingerprint.yaml file using a classical weighted term matching strategy (Salton & McGill, 1983). Enrichment will then be run on the highest-ranked candidate(s) from this stage. Lexical Coding can occur with or without a Language Model therefore even when no LM is installed, an ordered, inspectable result will be returned along with a set of scores. Stage two is contextual classification – paraphrase tolerant. Only the top ranked candidate(s) from Stage one will be fed into a Language Model (either Anthropic's Claude, or Google's Gemini, determined at runtime) via a few shot prompt that includes all relevant ring definitions, examples of high, medium, low exemplars, and negatives. The model is learning the boundaries for a match, instead of just a direct identification of the target (few shot prompts improve task adherence without fine tuning: Brown et al., "Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners", NeurIPS 33 (2020)). A valid JSON string MUST have all five required elements: a relevance score, the number of rings matched, thematic tags, a two-sentence summary annotation of the results, and a verbatim citable line. Any malformed output will trigger one additional round of stricter retries. If the model fails again after this single round of retries it will log classification_failed. Quota errors and/or network errors will cause the instrument to fall back to the lexical score therefore some sort of verdict will ALWAYS be produced. If the system does fall back, the item is flagged as such via a tag (keyword_fallback or classification_error_fallback), and the digest header reports the classifier as keyword-fallback rather than naming the model; readers can therefore always distinguish an LLM-written annotation from a purely lexical one. Because a language model can paraphrase a sentence and still present it as a quotation, the verbatim citable line is accepted only when it appears, character for character after whitespace and quotation marks are normalized, within the source text; a line that fails this check is discarded and the deterministic extractor supplies the quote instead. The few-shot prompt and this substring verification together bound the model's output, but they do not remove the need for human-in-the-loop review, and the digest annotations are meant to be verified by a reader before they are relied upon.

VI. Scoring, Calibration & Validation

A. Calibration decisions

A ring cannot be considered present until it has earned the right to be. It only counts once its lexical subtotal reaches a floor of twelve — enough to require a real hit on one or more terms rather than a brush against the surface of one. The rationale behind the choice of twelve was to filter out false positives — where a mining claim simply mentions an investment and could get misclassified as the theme; that minimum term-frequency cut-off (Manning et al. 2008) was intentional. The digest threshold of forty, meanwhile, sits twenty below the original figure. Neither value was arbitrary; both come from the natural distribution the deterministic scorer produces. When both on-theme and off-theme text were fed through the system, the cases fell into two distinctly different groups separated by a significant gap: labelled off-theme texts scored between zero and eight, with a mean of roughly one, while labelled on-theme texts scored between forty and seventy-five. No case in either of those two small corpora scored in the range of nine to thirty-nine. Later measurement does not reproduce that gap — the fourteen-probe suite in analytics/fingerprint_probes.json places four texts inside the band, two of them (one on-theme, one off-theme) at an identical thirty-three — so the separation is a property of the corpora measured rather than of the instrument. The forty sits within the lower bound of the on-theme cluster and the twelve sits within the dead-band of noise just above the off-theme scores. Both values were selected from direct observation, as opposed to intuition.

The instrument does not exercise judgment when it sends out results. It simply reports leads related to what was searched. Because the topics are narrow, many weeks will have very little content, and a reader could become conditioned to stop reviewing the digests after an extended stretch of near-empty weeks. To prevent that, the instrument is governed by a floor rather than dumping everything available. Any item scoring forty or higher on the scale is always reported to the user, and no ceiling exists on how many may be reported. In a week with fewer than six matches, the digest is filled toward six with the next-best candidates; no candidate scoring below twenty-five will ever be presented. Candidates brought forward this way are called watch-list leads, not matches — a user should never equate a lead with a holding. It is worth stating what that rule has actually produced. Across eleven archived runs the instrument has performed 347 screenings and returned no item at or above the match threshold; all fourteen items published to date are watch-list leads scoring between twenty-five and thirty-five. Both figures count events rather than distinct things, and the difference is worth stating: the seen-state was reset by hand twice during the first week, so items already screened re-entered the pool, and one italaw case page was published on two consecutive days, which makes the fourteen published entries thirteen distinct developments. The classifier returned contradictory verdicts on that duplicate — thirty-two with one ring on the first day, twenty-eight with none on the second, from the same URL and the same source text — so it falls on both sides of the ring count. Nine of the fourteen scored twenty-five and the rest between twenty-eight and thirty-five. Counted as entries, seven carry exactly one ring and seven carry none; counted as distinct developments, twelve of the thirteen carry a single verdict — six with a ring and six with none — and the thirteenth is the duplicate, which cannot be assigned to either side without arbitrarily preferring one of the two verdicts the instrument gave it. No published item has ever carried two rings. Those scores did not come from the deterministic scorer, which cannot produce twenty-five at all — with no ring at the presence floor its range is one to eleven. Every archived run was classified on the language-model path, where the presence floor is not applied and the model's own ring list is accepted without a consistency check against the score it reports. The fill-toward-six rule is therefore a source of noise as much as of continuity, and a watch-list lead should be read as a candidate the instrument could not rule out rather than one it affirmatively identified. And if a week passes in which no candidate meets the floor for relevance, the report says so plainly, along with the number of candidates evaluated against the topic.

B. Validation

The lexical scorer was tested at two levels, and neither level amounts to strong validation. Level one was a face-validity test against the seed examples in fingerprint.yaml. The scorer classified the seven labelled examples correctly on their own text, along with the three negative examples. That indicates the scorer can recognize the corpus that generated it. Level two asked whether the scorer had simply memorized the three positive seeds or had generalized a pattern, so I ran an exploratory holdout of twenty items using eval_holdout.py. Four of the twenty were on-theme positives — awards that took no part in the development process, namely Philip Morris v. Uruguay, Mondev v. United States, Loewen v. United States, and Apotex v. United States — and they were compared against sixteen other awards randomly pulled from the original data. Two of those four, Loewen and Mondev, involve no intellectual property at all, and the item captioned Apotex v. United States recites the jurisdictional holding of the 2013 Apotex Inc. award rather than that of the 2014 Apotex Holdings award its caption names. What this holdout measures is therefore whether the scorer separates adjacent investment-law material from off-theme material; it does not measure whether the scorer detects the three-ring intersection this memo is about. Evaluating at a score of forty, the scorer yielded a precision of 1.00, a recall of 0.75, and an accuracy of 0.95, for an F1 of 0.86; none of the off-theme awards was classified positively, and three of the four unknown on-theme awards were. Four further limits belong beside those figures. The labels are mine alone: there was no second independent coder and no measure of inter-rater agreement, and they were not assigned blind to the scorer's output. The holdout was not registered or locked before the digest threshold was chosen, so it cannot be read as a clean out-of-sample test of that threshold. Of the four positives only two, Loewen and Mondev, are verbatim award text; scripts/holdout_set.json records the Philip Morris v. Uruguay item as a paraphrase and the Apotex item as partial. And all sixteen negatives are live listings — institutional announcements, treaty-policy pieces, a staff vacancy — rather than hard cases such as an IP dispute with no treaty claim or an ISDS jurisdiction case with no IP, so a precision of 1.00 says little about the boundary cases that matter most. Given there were only four total positives in this evaluation, care is warranted in any claim about overall performance; the F1 of 0.86 in particular is illustrative rather than conclusive. That three of four unknown classifications registered at all suggests the fingerprint has some ability to generalize beyond its development corpus, but with a sample this small, that is little more than a suggestion.

VII. The Reporting Standard — The Digest as Annotated Bibliography

I selected the annotated bibliography as the digest format because the reporting standard called for interpretive description of the law, as opposed to simply summarizing it. Each entry accordingly contains both a citation and a descriptive-and-evaluative annotation. Entries generally run 50-200 words per the AALL Law Library Journal guidelines, carry references in the style of The Chicago Manual of Style, and provide a URL so readers can locate the source. Every entry assesses the relevance of the item to the overall project and, if relevant, how. Each links back to the originating document and, where the body of that document is accessible, includes at least one direct verbatim quote from it, giving the reader enough to find the source without additional searching. Where the body is not accessible — a paywalled or headline-only item — no quotation is manufactured; the entry records the access limit in place of a quote. And any limitation on accessing the original — paywall, login, metadata-only — is clearly noted, never concealed.

VIII. The Interpretive Companion — The Research Council & the Research Brief

The ISDS Research Brief is also generated in addition to the digest in the same cycle. This brief is produced by a multi-stage AI-assisted classification and review workflow. Each stage is a separately invoked agent bound to its own instruction prompt and to the model configured in src/models.py — the chairman and research analyst on Claude Opus 5, the remaining seats on Claude Opus 4.8, though the agent definitions pin a model tier rather than a version — convened daily by the chairman, who directs the analysis but never writes any stage's contribution. These passes are not independent researchers: they share source material, prompt lineage, model family, and framing assumptions, and can therefore share the same errors, so agreement between them is not corroboration; a security officer independently re-verifies high-risk elements, and its objections bind what the editor may publish. The Chairman sets the weekly agenda by threading forward. The Analyst evaluates the developments that have been screened relative to the research question; the Analyst proposes each of the substantive claims as a structured candidate. Every proposed claim must then pass a deterministic integrity gate that verifies it, by exact identifier, against my append-only verification ledger; in parallel, a deterministic citation check machine-verifies each cited source; and an editor creates the final product. While that final document is coming together, the analyst may conduct web searches to deepen insight on developments already screened — but web search cannot be used as a second collection channel. The same limit governs the runner-relayed fetch: it may retrieve documents already identified by the curated sources or already named in the record, and may not be used to discover new material. Each of these agents works under a binding calibration checklist that is meant to catch the failures models are most known for: made-up citations or quotes, claims that reach past the evidence provided, and relevance that gets overstated. Any high-risk element; citations, quotes, holdings, dates, figures; has to be checked against its real source before it can be asserted, or else it gets marked unavailable instead. That pre-publication check is the anti-hallucination workflow, and the deterministic ledger gate sits in front as gatekeeper while the citation check backs it up. There is also an analytics piece that watches which catalogue sources have actually been responsive to the thematic intersection, and through this means the coverage can be adapted over time based on which feeds are actually producing content. The full council meets every day. Its weekly reconvening results in two documents; the brief and the chairman's candid accountability assessment. As such, the analysis does not start over when a day ends; instead the analyst keeps a living state-of-the-answer synthesis of what is currently known, plus an append-only, deduplicated insight ledger where each session gets compared against the knowledge that existed before it. A recurring human-review checkpoint spot-audits a sample of the claims cited over the period, and the outcomes are logged in HUMAN_REVIEW.md. That formal checkpoint has run once, on 18 July 2026, but it is not the whole of the review I have done: the append-only ledger at analytics/verification_ledger.jsonl records twenty-one claims I verified against their sources on 27 July 2026, each tied to a claim identifier and to the access status of the document consulted, on top of the claims registered there on 18, 20 and 21 July. What has not happened is a human legal classification of the digest entries themselves; those remain machine-screened and should be read as such. The brief, the deliberation of the council, and the accountability record are all archived together with each digest.

IX. Reproducibility & State

Every item passed through this instrument is recorded into a state file named state/seen.json - one entry for each source and each unique id generated. The pipeline will start over if it cannot recover from a bad state file but will never stop. In addition to the above, each scheduled run is documented in a version controlled repository, with a copy of every digest by date as well as a snapshot of the present state - all the things that have been collected and their associated scores - essentially the same as documenting your own manually annotated observations.

X. Limitations

The status should be stated before the detail: this is an exploratory research prototype. Over eleven archived runs it has screened 347 candidates and identified no direct match at or above the threshold, and its validation remains preliminary in the specific senses set out in Part VI.B. There are limitations of coverage and access for this project and I make them clear at the outset, so the reader will know just how much of the field the tool can see. The thematic constructs are limited in terms of their construct validity due to the fact that there were only three seeds for the development of this theme; therefore the number of doctrinal variants those seeds produce may be an underestimate. Although the weight of extension terms is lower than the seed terms, adjacent doctrines such as Denial of Benefits and Substantial Business Activity remain reachable by the tool; that lessens the limitation without removing it. The use of a most similar case approach with three seeds also creates a possibility that the results would reflect overfitting. The small exploratory hold-out set in Part VI.B — twenty items, only four positives — represents at least a partial answer, since three of the four unseen awards were correctly identified. The remainder is a legitimate concern: four confirmatory cases can never exhaust all the language possibilities the instrument's structure must handle. Furthermore, the hold out set is likely skewed toward NAFTA and ICSID denial-of-justice awards (Loewen and Mondev utilize more of the judicial measure ring than the full IP-as-investment intersection), and thus Ring 1 is utilized substantially less independently than Rings 2 and 3, so a pure IP-as-investment case with no judicial measure component would represent the instrument's most likely false negative. No claim beyond face-plus-holdout validity can be made until the scorer is validated against a much larger human-coded sample of non-English and non-NAFTA awards. Two implementation simplifications belong in this register for the same reason. The lexical matcher uses case-insensitive substrings rather than lemmas, and holds no truncated stems, so an unusual form of a trigger term may avoid being recognized. And since duplicates are eliminated per source using a stable id, a dispute appearing via multiple feeds is resolved downstream at the Research Brief's insight ledger rather than being merged during intake. Sources that produce only headlines — IAReporter among them — often de-rate the most genuinely on-theme cases, where the decisive information sits inside a paywalled body the tool cannot access; those items appear as watch list leads rather than matches. Coverage is a floor. Paywalls and robots.txt can withhold content, so the instrument shows the least that can exist — not a complete census. The Research Brief presents an additional risk that is distinct from the ones above: it utilizes model generated interpretations and, through bounded web searches, material outside of the curated corpus, both of which can create citation hallucinations or exaggerate holdings — reduced not removed by the council's verification process and the deliberate constraint placed on searching — so the interpretations and supplemental findings contained within the Research Brief are provided as research leads that need to be validated prior to their utilization and not as conclusions.

A further limitation concerns the verification status of the council's interpretive output. Since July 2026 the Research Brief may assert a finding only if the operator has personally verified the underlying claim in the append-only verification ledger (an exact-identifier lookup; a reachable source URL is never treated as evidence the claim is true); everything not so verified is presented as an unverified lead or routed for library access. This restricts what the brief can state plainly, and it means the assertable record grows no faster than my own capacity to review — a trade of coverage for reliability that I made deliberately. Additionally, a re-weighting of the Ring 1 lexicon toward the trade-secret and clinical-data vocabulary was approved and applied in July 2026, raising that cluster to the dominant weight in the ring while preserving sensitivity to the patent and trademark case shapes; the approval and its terms are recorded in working/FINGERPRINT_DRIFT.md, which also notes that the weighting is to be revisited upward once a decided trade-secret ISDS case converts the hypothesized pathway into case law.
