## I. Research Question & Scope
This memo sets out the operational methodology of the ISDS Thematic Watcher — an automated monitoring tool I built for this project using Claude AI and Claude Code as the development medium. Every operational choice in its development was grounded in the literature reviewed to date. Beyond documenting the methodology, the memo speaks to one of the project's substantive questions: How can changes in investor-State dispute settlement practice be identified, over time and within a particular area of law, where an intellectual property right is asserted as a covered investment, a governmental act or court decision is challenged as a violation of that right, and a rule of law applied at the jurisdictional or admissibility level determines that the case cannot proceed — and how can that be done repeatedly and uniformly? Rather than resolve the substance of those issues — whether trade secrets and clinical trial data qualify as protected investments — this memo addresses the methodological approach used to identify the areas of law that meet those criteria; for the substantive arguments on what rights are entitled to protection under international investment agreements, the Ferguson and Kim memos should be consulted. What follows is how the ISDS Thematic Watcher turns that legal framework into a repeatable process with multiple points of auditability, including contestation where necessary. The memo proceeds through the theoretical basis of the instrument (Part II), its source architecture and sourcing mechanism (Part III), candidate identification and enrichment (Part IV), a two-stage classification cascade (Part V), scoring, calibration, and validation (Part VI), the reporting standard for the digest (Part VII), reproducibility (Part VIII), and a candid account of limitations (Part IX).


## II. Theoretical Framework — The Three-Ring Thematic Fingerprint

### A. Why a thematic instrument, and not a keyword search
What is under surveillance is not a single term but a relationship among three doctrines — and that relationship cannot be reduced to one keyword without losing the substance of what it is. A case might use the word "patent" a hundred times and still be irrelevant; another might never say "abuse of right" and yet fit the category exactly. The problem, in other words, is interpretive rather than lexical, which is precisely the kind of problem qualitative method was built for: how do you train a tool to recognize a category when it has only three instances to learn from? Treating the fingerprint's development as an exercise in theory-informed sampling and most-similar-case design — which Linos & Carlson urge on legal scholars, since legal authors tend to pick examples unsystematically and analyze them in purely doctrinal terms (Linos & Carlson, Qualitative Methods for Law Review Writing, 84 U. Chi. L. Rev. 213 (2017)) — I did not build the seed corpus to represent all of ISDS. I built it from structural similarity along the dimensions of interest, following the logic of a most-similar-case method.

### B. The seed corpus
The three awards selected represent the three forms of arbitration awards that have the same type of structure in all three subject matter areas. Each award has the structural elements outlined by the project for identifying those structural elements. In Philip Morris Asia Ltd. v. Commonwealth of Australia, PCA Case No. 2012-12, Award on Jurisdiction and Admissibility (Dec. 17, 2015), Philip Morris made a claim against an Australian statute which required tobacco products to be sold with blank packaging claiming its treaty based Intellectual Property Protections. The Arbitral Tribunal denied the Claim as an Abuse of Right because Philip Morris had created a new entity in Hong Kong so that it could obtain Treaty Rights in regard to a foreseen dispute. In Eli Lilly & Company v. Government of Canada, ICSID Case No. UNCT/14/2, Final Award (March 16, 2017), Eli Lilly challenged the Courts of Canada's decision to invalidate Eli Lilly's patents using the "Promise Utility Doctrine", which was a judicial tool used to invalidate the patents and Eli Lilly claimed that the invalidations violated Article 1105 of NAFTA's Minimum Standard of Treatment, which defined Denial of Justice. Unlike Philip Morris, this case was decided on the merits. The Arbitral Tribunal determined there was no drastic change in Canadian Patent Law and did not find anything arbitrary or discriminatory in the utility requirement (Eli Lilly & Co. v. Canada, Final Award (Mar. 16, 2017), italaw case 1625, https://www.italaw.com/cases/1625; IISD, Investment Treaty News (June 12, 2017), https://www.iisd.org/itn/2017/06/12/highly-anticipated-nafta-award-rejects-patent-law-related-claim-against-canada-matthew-levine/). In Bridgestone Licensing Services, Inc. & Bridgestone Americas, Inc. v. Republic of Panama, ICSID Case No. ARB/16/34, Award (August 14, 2020), Bridgestone claimed that a Judgment entered by the Supreme Court of Panama constituted a Denial of Justice. It also raised the operating entity versus holding company question, i.e. whether the Trademark Licenses were a Covered Investment -- jurisdictional/standing issues similar to the Corporate Structure Issues involved in Philip Morris Asia Ltd. However, the Arbitral Tribunal primarily ruled in favor of the Claimants regarding that threshold question during the Expedited Objections phase and thereafter denied both the Denial of Justice and Fair and Equitable Treatment Claims on the Merits concluding there was no Denial of Justice and no demonstrated harm (Bridgestone v. Panama, Award (Aug. 14, 2020), italaw case 4475, https://www.italaw.com/cases/4475; Decision on Expedited Objections (Dec. 13, 2017), https://jusmundi.com/en/document/decision/en-bridgestone-americas-inc-and-bridgestone-licensing-services-inc-v-republic-of-panama-decision-on-expedited-objections-wednesday-13th-december-2017). The Index Unit for all awards is the Arbitrators' language itself, i.e. the operational phrases used were directly extracted from each award as shown by the full Per-Ring Extraction in PLAN.md. This selection was intentional and corresponds to one of the basic principles of information retrieval: The best terms for distinguishing among relevant documents will always be those appearing within those documents themselves (Salton & McGill, Introduction to Modern Information Retrieval (1983); Manning et al., Introduction to Information Retrieval (2008)).

The two basic concepts of the literature review -- the memos from Ferguson and Kim can be seen in their full form at lit-review/ -- were the basis of the research question and will serve as the base for Ring 1; however, they did not represent a fourth case, rather they complemented the three arbitration awards. In Trade Secrets at Risk, Alexander Ferguson stated that trade secrets are likely to be the most vulnerable category of intellectual property and further stated that there already exists an adequate amount of protections for trade secrets as covered investments under existing regimes when the regime is properly interpreted: he defines the asset through TRIPS Article 39 and VCLT Article 31(3)(c) and states that this protection is threatened by a four part risk taxonomy (disclosure, use, regulation, and combinations of these), and also provides a four-question indirect expropriation test -- does the state's protection have to be absolute? Has the state caused a significant interference with the legal protection of the secret, with the secret's exclusive commercial use, or a significant decrease in the commercial value of the secret? All of this is based on a recognition/enforceability gap which represents a structural issue: International law only protects that which municipal law of the host state currently recognizes. Daria Kim's Protecting Trade Secrets under International Investment Law is the direct counterpoint: reasoning from pharmaceutical clinical trial data, she argues that such data is unlikely to win protection through ISDS — because clinical data is structurally unusual at the "investment" threshold (the Apotex v. United States decision marks the outer limit of "investment," while Philip Morris v. Uruguay applies the Salini contribution-to-development criterion as a discretionary jurisdictional backstop); because the expropriation standard creates an almost impossible counterfactual evidentiary burden whenever the disclosure is non-commercial regulation; because the "legitimate expectations" route through fair-and-equitable-treatment is too vague to imply confidentiality; and because TRIPS Article 39(3) is an obligation against unfair commercial use rather than a general prohibition on disclosure, which lets the proportionality and police-powers carve-out treat a public-health disclosure as non-compensable. The actual disagreement between Ferguson and Kim — his optimistic interpretation against her structural skepticism over exactly the same treaty architecture (BITs, the ICSID Convention, TRIPS Article 39, and the VCLT) — provides the core framework for the research question. And it is from these two memos, not from any single award, that Ring 1 obtains both its working vocabulary (trade secret, clinical trial data, test data, data exclusivity, unfair commercial use, TRIPS Article 39(3)) and the doctrinal stakes that justify surfacing a development at all.

### C. The three rings
The first ring — intellectual property as a protected investment. This ring deals with whether, and under what conditions, intellectual property should be considered a "covered investment" — the defining issue that repeatedly arises in the reading materials of the project (Mercurio, Awakening the Sleeping Giant, 15 J. Int'l Econ. L. 871 (2012); Correa & Viñuales, Intellectual Property Rights as Protected Investments: How Open are the Gates?, 19 J. Int'l Econ. L. 91 (2016)). This ring has the greatest strength when the asset is information based, when there is ownership divided between a holding and an operating company such as in Bridgestone, and when it shares the structural vulnerability identified by the Ferguson and Kim memos for trade secrets and clinical trial data. The defining issue of this ring also incorporates the Salini criteria of contribution, duration, risk, and contribution to the host economy — which provide tribunals with a significant degree of discretion (compare Salini Costruttori S.p.A. v. Morocco, ICSID Case No. ARB/00/4). Triggers for ring one include: covered investment; promise utility doctrine; exploitation of the trademark.
Ring Two – Regulatory/Judicial Action as the Challenged Behavior (Weighted). Two of the three seeds were brought against domestic courts rather than government agency action, and both alleged a violation of procedural due process resulting in a denial of justice. The standards for finding a state liable for its judiciary are articulated in Paulsson, Denial of Justice in International Law (2005): a State may only be found liable for the decisions of its judiciary upon a demonstration of manifest injustice; mere mistake does not meet that burden. The seed tribunals use essentially the same terms to articulate their findings of liability — conduct that was outrageous and egregious, manifestly unjust judgments, rulings so erroneous that no reasonable or competent tribunal could have rendered them. Since the seed corpus places such a heavy reliance on this ring, the weight assigned to this ring is commensurately high: if a claimant alleges a wrongfully issued court decision they may reach the MEDIUM level on that allegation alone. This was a deliberate trade-off made at the scoring-design stage and not an oversight: over-weighting the judicial-measure ring does risk under-reading an IP-as-investment case that reaches the threshold by a purely administrative route, but because that ring on its own only ever reaches MEDIUM and never HIGH, it widens recall without letting any single doctrine manufacture a top-band hit, and the residual blind spot is carried forward openly in Part IX. In addition to these acknowledged methods of determining categories' weights based on frequency of claims within a coding corpus (Saldana, Coding Manual), there are several other recognized methods. Triggers for ring two include: denial of justice; manifestly unjust judgment; minimum standard of treatment.
Ring Three – Threshold Questions regarding Jurisdiction and Admissibility as a Potentially Dispositive Dimension. This ring observes the ground on which a case may be denied disposition prior to having its merits addressed: abuse of right, treaty shopping, foreseeability of the dispute, restructuring for treaty protection, and the disputed definitions of "the investor" and/or "the investment." Among the seeds, this dimension was primarily established through Philip Morris v. Australia — the only case that was decided based on it (held inadmissible as an abuse of right without addressing the merits). It played a secondary role in Bridgestone where the standing/covered investment question was answered mostly for the claimants, and thus the merits of the case determined the outcome; and it did not form part of the reasoning in Eli Lilly — which was decided on the merits. The point here is that these preliminary questions may act as dispositive issues — and did in Philip Morris — even though all three seeds were not decided based on these issues. The most recent authority used to develop the integration is Baumgartner, Treaty Shopping in International Investment Law (2016) — which expands upon an earlier abuse-of-right principle (Byers, Abuse of Rights: An Old Principle, A New Age, 47 McGill L.J. 389 (2002)) — alongside the standards provided in the leading texts (Dolzer & Schreuer, Principles of International Investment Law (2nd ed. 2012); McLachlan et al., International Investment Arbitration: Substantive Principles (2nd ed. 2017)). Triggers for ring three include: abuse of right; reasonably foreseeable; critical date; shell subsidiary.

### D. The scoring grammar
The scoring grammar represents what the tool will be able to identify as the crossing of the bands. HIGH (70+), requires 2 rings. MEDIUM (40-69), can take either a very strong first ring that has a much weaker second ring, or it can stand alone using the judicial measure. LOW (< 40), takes one weak ring or no rings. Extractives, Sovereign Debt, and Intra-EU Energy Disputes are automatically assigned a score of LOW unless there exists an IP or Judicial angle. The complete machine readable format for all phrases with their respective weights that add up to 100 per ring is located in fingerprint.yaml. Each phrase is matched as a case-insensitive substring of the item's combined title, summary, and body text rather than as a whole word, so a trigger fires regardless of the words around it; where a term inflects heavily the fingerprint registers a truncated stem (for instance "expropriat-") to catch the variants, although it stops short of full lemmatization or regular-expression patterns, which Part IX records as a known simplification.


## III. Source Architecture & Research Methods
I used a thoughtful strategy when selecting my resources and testing their credibility. I have also deliberately selected multiple types of resource. This enables me to obtain a range of information regarding investment disputes, and I have structured this around the four main ways in which I can find information about such disputes -- institutional documentation, archives, analyses and practitioner commentaries. The open repositories I accessed include: the ICSID docket; UNCTAD's Investment Dispute Settlement Navigator and World Investment Report; the italaw primary-document archive; IISD's Investment Treaty News; and IAReporter at headline level. In addition to these channels, I added two further channels to access additional information. They included: the operator's own Google Alerts feeds (polled as RSS) to track both pending and "seed" case developments and Google Scholar alerts (read from a designated mailbox) to identify new academic work that focuses upon the seed cases and doctrines. There are three levels of how much I was able to access. Full read -- I was able to read in full the ICSID docket, UNCTAD, italaw, IISD Investment Treaty News and the operator's own Google Alerts and Google Scholar feeds (as the publishers allow). Headline-only read -- IAReporter, who only publishes titles and leads because they place their bodies behind a paywall. Disabled -- Google News RSS (currently disabled due to a block placed in its robots.txt file), and any individual web-page that has been denied by either a robots.txt rule or a log-in wall. As a result of the above tiers, the instrument should be viewed as a lead generation floor, i.e., the bare minimum number of significant events that can be generated using publicly accessible text, rather than being capable of generating a complete census of all ISDS activities at the intersection. The underlying rationale for the development of this instrument was based upon triangulation -- cross-checking between different sources (based on the assumption that results that appear in the same way in two or more independent sources are more likely to represent an actual pattern than a random occurrence). The above mentioned repositories are also the ones I use manually when back-sourcing; however the tool simply does this process simultaneously with regard to all of them rather than individually. The technical method employed for collecting information involves extracting items according to pre-defined rules: Once it finds listings, it retrieves the original full-text version of the proceeding, and records the reference for each item, including the access date/time. That stable identifier is also what prevents duplication: the seen-state file (state/seen.json) keys every processed item by its source and id, so the same listing is never scored twice. Where a single dispute surfaces across several feeds, those copies are reconciled later at the Research Brief's deduplicated insight ledger rather than at intake; fully merging one dispute across feeds at the scoring stage — by matching party names, dates, and case numbers — remains a known limitation noted in Part IX. With respect to privacy and accessibility issues, I did not collect anything from behind a subscription/pay-wall or authentication requirement; I follow robot exclusion protocols, I wait at least three seconds before accessing content on the same domain; and I identify myself in the User-Agent header string. The one possible exception is that of the operator's own subscribed Google Alerts feeds which Google provides for the operator to poll within a feed-reader.


## IV. Candidate Identification & Enrichment
Candidate names are listed and classified based on their content; this is why enrichment should be performed prior to classification. The best candidates for enrichment are identified by stripping each candidate's source document down to its readable form, then scoring every full sentence in that readable text for doctrinal density: that score counts how many fingerprint keywords the sentence carries (weighted the most heavily), then how many holding verbs it uses (held, found, dismissed, and the like), then evaluative terms (egregious, manifest, denial), while date-heavy and all-capitals lines are thrown out so paragraph numbers and case captions cannot win. The readable body is what the classifier then evaluates; the single highest-scoring sentence is carried forward as the candidate citable line. Just like how a properly placed pin citation allows researchers to quickly locate the appropriate reference from a well-researched bibliography through back sourcing, that line gives the reader at least one sentence worth reading and the digest a verbatim quotation to anchor the entry.


## V. Classification Procedure — A Two-Stage Cascade
Classification occurs in two stages – both of which have advantages and disadvantages in terms of their precision/recall. Stage one is lexical coding – deterministic, and transparent. All candidates are ranked based on the keyword weights contained within the fingerprint.yaml file using a classical weighted term matching strategy (Salton & McGill, 1983). Enrichment will then be run on the highest-ranked candidate(s) from this stage. Lexical Coding can occur with or without a Language Model therefore even when no LM is installed, an ordered, inspectable result will be returned along with a set of scores. Stage two is contextual classification – paraphrase tolerant. Only the top ranked candidate(s) from Stage one will be fed into a Language Model (either Anthropic's Claude, or Google's Gemini, determined at runtime) via a few shot prompt that includes all relevant ring definitions, examples of high, medium, low exemplars, and negatives. The model is learning the boundaries of what constitutes a match rather than simply identifying the target (few shot prompts improve task adherence without fine tuning: Brown et al., "Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners", NeurIPS 33 (2020)). The model MUST produce a valid JSON string. A valid JSON string contains: relevance score, number of rings matched, thematic tags, a two sentence summary/annotation of the results, and a verbatim citable line. Any malformed output will trigger ONE additional round of stricter retries. If the model fails again after this single round of retries it will log classification_failed. Quota errors and/or network errors will cause the instrument to fall back to the lexical score therefore some sort of verdict will ALWAYS be produced. When that fallback occurs the item is tagged for it (keyword_fallback or classification_error_fallback) and the digest header reports the classifier as keyword-fallback rather than naming the model, so that a reader can always tell an LLM-written annotation from a raw lexical one. Because a language model can paraphrase a sentence and still present it as a quotation, the verbatim citable line is accepted only when it appears, character for character after whitespace and quotation marks are normalized, within the source text; a line that fails this check is discarded and the deterministic extractor supplies the quote instead. The few-shot prompt and this substring verification together bound the model's output, but they do not remove the need for human-in-the-loop review, and the digest annotations are meant to be verified by a reader before they are relied upon.


## VI. Scoring, Calibration & Validation

### A. Calibration decisions
A ring can't be considered as present until it has earned its right to be. It can only count if its Lexical Subtotal reaches a floor of twelve -- enough to have to produce some type of "hit" on one or more terms instead of simply brushing against the surface of one term. The idea behind this is to eliminate what could be called false positives -- such as when a mining claim merely refers to "an investment," and it gets confused with a theme; the Minimum Term Frequency Cut-Off (Manning et al. 2008) was chosen intentionally. The Digest Threshold was established at forty, which is twenty less than the original figure. Both numbers are not arbitrarily selected -- they were taken directly from the Score Distribution that the Deterministic Scorer would naturally produce. When run over substantial text (both on-theme and off-theme), cases fell into two distinct clusters with a large empty space between them: labelled off-theme texts produced scores ranging from zero to eight, with a mean value of just under one; labelled on-theme texts produced scores ranging from forty to seventy-five. There is no case where a score falls within the range of nine to thirty-nine. Thus the forty is located at the bottom of the On-Theme Cluster and the Ring-Present Floor of Twelve is found in the Dead Band directly above the Off-Theme Noise Space -- both derived by observation as opposed to intuition.

The instrument does not make any kind of judgment when it sends out the results of a search. It merely sends you "leads" about what you've searched for. Therefore, even though the topics are somewhat specific, many times a week will be completely blank because the instrument has only sent one lead. This can result in a reader being trained to stop reading these digests when they have been completely blank for some time. To avoid this from happening, the instrument is deliberately limited by a floor level rather than sending out all of the data available. All items that score forty or higher on the scale will always be reported back to the user. There is no ceiling established for reporting back to the users. If there are less than six matches during a particular week, the users' digest will be filled up (toward six) with the next best matching candidate(s). However, these next best matches must also score twenty-five or better. No candidate that scores lower than twenty-five will ever be presented to the user. Candidates that are brought forward through this process are referred to as "watch-list leads," rather than "matches." Therefore, a user should never confuse a lead with a holding. Additionally, if a week occurs where no candidate meets the floor criteria for relevance, then the report will clearly indicate that fact, along with the number of candidates who were reviewed against the topic.

### B. Validation
I tested the lexical scorer on two different levels; neither level reached a strong form of validation. Level 1: face validity test—tested against seed examples in fingerprint.yaml. The lexical scorer correctly classified the seven labelled examples and the three negative examples. All seven were correctly classified on their own text. This shows that the lexical scorer is able to recognize the corpus that generated it (Linos & Carlson; Saldana). Level 2: to see if the lexical scorer simply memorized the three positive seeds or has generalized a pattern of classification, I ran an exploratory holdout of twenty items using eval_holdout.py. There were only four of these twenty items that are on theme positives. These on theme positives were four awards that did not take part in the development process (Philip Morris v. Uruguay, Mondev v. United States, Loewen v. United States, and Apotex v. United States), and they were compared to sixteen other awards that were randomly pulled from the original data. When evaluating the results at a score of forty, the lexical scorer yielded a precision of 1.00, a recall of 0.75, and an accuracy of 0.95 (F1 of 0.86); none of the off-theme awards were classified positively and three of the four unknown on-theme awards were classified positively. Since there were only four total positives in this evaluation, we should be careful when making any claims about overall performance; the F1 of 0.86 in particular is illustrative, not conclusive. Since Loewen and Mondev utilize the judicial-measure ring much more often than the entire IP-as-investment intersection, we can consider the hold out as testing Ring 2 more independently than Ring 1. As for why Apotex failed, it was due to a holding by the court that the regulatory rights of the claimant were not a covered investment, which means that this type of case would be missed by any deterministic scoring model. That points to a tuning direction rather than a dead end: because negative-threshold rulings matter to the research question as much as successful assertions do, the paraphrase-tolerant language-model stage is where they are meant to be recovered, and adding explicit non-investment phrasings ("not a covered investment") to the fingerprint would let the lexical stage flag such rejections as well. The fact that three out of four unknown classifications registered at all suggests that the fingerprint has some ability to generalize beyond the development corpus; however, given the size of the sample, this is little more than a suggestion.


## VII. The Reporting Standard — The Digest as Annotated Bibliography
The annotated bibliography was selected as the format to be used to create the digest since the reporting standard required the writer to provide an interpretive description of the law and not just a summary of the law. In the same style as an annotated bibliography each entry contains both a citation and a descriptive-and-evaluative annotation. Entries were written under the AALL Law Library Journal length guidelines (50-200 words) using citations in The Chicago Manual of Style and contained a URL to assist the reader to find the source. All entries evaluate how relevant items are in terms of what developments relate to this project and, if they do, how. Each entry identifies the source, provides a link to the original content and includes a quote of a specific verbatim line from the source, thus providing sufficient information for the reader to obtain the source material by themselves; i.e., no further search will be necessary. If access to the original is restricted in some way (paywall, login requirement, metadata only), then such limitations are noted and not concealed.

### The Interpretive Companion — The Research Council & the Research Brief
Along with the digest the instrument also generates an interpretative companion document within the same cycle as the digest: the ISDS Research Brief. This document is produced by a small council of role-bound stages, each stage trained to its task through either a dedicated instruction prompt or deterministic check. These role-bound stages consist of a chairman responsible for determining the week's agenda using forward threads, an analyst who interprets the screened developments against the research question, a security officer who compares the analyst's work against a binding calibration checklist, a deterministic integrity check that machine-verifies all cited sources, and finally an editor who produces the final document. In producing the final document, the analyst may conduct web search, however only to deepen insight on already-screened developments, never as a second collection channel. The binding calibration checklist enforced by the security officer protects every issue from the familiar model failure modes of fabricated citations or quotations, over reach beyond evidence presented, and inflated relevance. Under the calibration checklist, every high-risk element (a citation, quote, holding, date, figure etc.) must be verified against its real source before it can be asserted or marked unavailable; this pre-publication verification is the anti-hallucination workflow with the security officer as its gate and the deterministic citation check as its back stop. That back stop is not the model auditing itself: it is a model-free script that extracts every http(s) URL from the brief and actually fetches each one over the network, marking any that fail to resolve as a suspected fabrication, while URL-less authorities — law-review articles, treatises, and awards cited by case number — are flagged for human verification rather than silently trusted. Separately, an analytics function tracks which catalogue sources are receptive to the intersection of the developing themes, therefore coverage can be tuned over time toward those feeds that actually deliver. The full council meets daily and their weekly reconvene produces both the brief and the chairman's candid accountability assessment. Thus the work compounds rather than restarting each day: the analyst maintains a living state-of-the-answer synthesis together with an append-only deduplicated insight ledger where each session measures new findings against prior ones. A recurring human-review checkpoint spot-audits a sample of periodically cited claims for the purpose of logging outcome. The brief, the deliberation of the council, and that accountability record are archived with each digest.


## VIII. Reproducibility & State
Every item that comes through the instrument is saved with source and unique ID to a state file called state/seen.json so everything is processed once and never again. The state files are recoverable even if they become corrupted because the pipeline will start over instead of stopping. Each scheduled run is saved to a version-controlled repository along with a copy of all digests for each date and a snapshot of the current state (all items collected and their corresponding score) — this provides the same type of documentation that is provided using manual annotation to document the original observations.


## IX. Limitations
There are limitations of coverage and access for this project and I make them clear at the outset, so the reader will know just how much of the field the tool can see. In terms of construct validity: the theme was developed from three awards, therefore the number of doctrinal variants that these seeds produce may be an underestimate. The Theme-Extension Terms are assigned low weights to allow adjacent doctrines ("Denial of Benefits", "Substantial Business Activity") to be reached by the tool, but this does not eliminate the limitation, only mitigates it. The most similar case method based on three seeds creates an opportunity to argue that the results are due to overfitting. As such, the use of a small exploratory hold-out set in Part VI.B, a total of twenty items with only four positive hits, represents at least part of the solution to the problem, since three of the four unseen awards were correctly identified. The remaining risk is legitimate: four confirmatory cases could never possibly exhaust all of the language possibilities in the structure of the instrument. Furthermore, the hold out set is likely skewed toward NAFTA and ICSID denial-of-justice awards (Loewen and Mondev utilize more of the judicial measure ring than the full IP-as-investment intersection), and thus Ring 1 is utilized substantially less independently than Rings 2 and 3, so a pure IP-as-investment case with no judicial measure component would represent the instrument's most likely false negative. Validation using a larger human coded sample of non English and non NAFTA awards is required before any claims greater than face plus holdout validity can be made. Two implementation simplifications belong here for the same reason: the lexical matcher operates on case-insensitive substrings and a handful of truncated stems rather than on full lemmatization, so an unusual inflection of a trigger term can be missed; and de-duplication is performed per source by stable id, so a single dispute arriving through several different feeds is only reconciled downstream at the Research Brief's insight ledger rather than merged at intake. Coverage is a floor. Content can be withheld via pay walls and robots.txt, and because Google News RSS is currently blocked and inactive, the instrument will show the least amount possible to exist versus creating a complete census. Sources utilizing headlines only — IAReporter in particular — tend to down score truly on theme cases when the dispositive detail resides in a pay-walled body that the instrument is unable to read, so those items will surface as watch list leads versus matches. The Research Brief presents an additional risk that is distinct from the ones above: it utilizes model generated interpretations and, through bounded web searches, material outside of the curated corpus, both of which can create citation hallucinations or exaggerate holdings — reduced not removed by the council's verification process and the deliberate constraint placed on searching — so the interpretations and supplemental findings contained within the Research Brief are provided as research leads that need to be validated prior to their utilization and not as conclusions.
